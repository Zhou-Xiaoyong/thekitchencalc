## 500 Grams of Flour at 70% Takes 350 Grams of Water

Bowl on the scale, tare, 500 g of bread flour. The water line is one multiplication away: 500 × 0.70 = **350 g**. Weigh 350 g of water into that bowl and the dough is at 70% hydration.

That is the whole answer, and it is worth knowing why it feels too short. Most recipes hide the number inside a list of cups, and most bakers end up adding water by feel because nobody told them the flour weight is the only figure that matters. Two numbers and a decimal, and the guesswork is gone.

## The Formula, Written Out Once

**Water weight = flour weight × hydration percentage ÷ 100**

Run it forward and 500 g at 70% is 350 g. Run it backward and 500 g of water at 70% needs 714 g of flour. Everything else in this article is that equation applied to a different starting point.

Here is what the 500 g batch looks like as a complete formula with salt and yeast in it:

| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 500 g | 100% |
| Water | 350 g | 70% |
| Fine sea salt | 10 g | 2% |
| Active dry yeast | 6 g | 1.2% |
| **Total** | **866 g** | **173.2%** |

The 866 g at the bottom is the dough weight, and it is the number that decides whether this batch fits your pan. A 9x5 inch loaf pan wants 800 to 850 g of dough, so 500 g of flour overshoots by about 20 g. Drop to 491 g of flour, 344 g of water, and 9.8 g of salt and the same dough fills the pan to the right height. The percentage does not move. Only the flour line does.

## What the 500 Grams Has to Mean

The 350 g answer is only correct if 500 g is the total flour in the dough, and it usually is not what the bowl contains.

Anything that already carries flour counts toward the 500. That includes a poolish, a biga, a levain, and the whole wheat portion of a blend. Skip this and the water number stays right while the hydration quietly climbs.

A worked case. You want 500 g of total flour at 70%, and the recipe calls for a 100 g poolish at 100% hydration:

- The poolish is 50 g flour and 50 g water.
- Flour to scoop: 500 − 50 = **450 g**.
- Water to pour: 350 − 50 = **300 g**.

Check it before mixing: 300 + 50 = 350 g of water, 450 + 50 = 500 g of flour, and 350 ÷ 500 = **70.0%**. Same target, two very different sets of numbers on the counter. The [Sourdough Calculator](/sourdough-calculator/) does this subtraction for you when the pre-ferment is a starter rather than a poolish, since a starter's flour and water have to come out of the totals the same way.

## 350 Grams of Water in a Measuring Cup

Water is the one ingredient where grams and milliliters are interchangeable: 350 g is 350 mL, near enough for any kitchen.

Our [conversion chart](/conversion-chart/) uses 240 g per cup for water, so 350 g is **1.46 cups**, or 1 cup plus 7 tablespoons. By volume in fluid ounces that is about 11.8 fl oz. Close is fine here, because a 5 g error in water moves a 500 g batch by one percentage point.

| Flour | Water at 70% | Water by volume (240 g/cup) | Finished dough |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 g | 175 g | ¾ cup minus 1 teaspoon | 433 g |
| 500 g | 350 g | 1 cup + 7 tbsp | 866 g |
| 750 g | 525 g | 2 cups + 3 tbsp | 1,299 g |
| 1,000 g | 700 g | 2⅞ cups | 1,732 g |

Doubling is the only operation in that table. Two 500 g batches are 1,000 g of flour and 700 g of water, and nothing in the method changes.

## Going the Other Way: From a Dough Weight to 350 Grams

Sometimes the flour weight is the unknown. You have a pan, or a target loaf size, and the water follows from the dough weight.

**Flour = dough weight ÷ 1.732**, where 1.732 is the sum of flour at 100%, water at 70%, salt at 2%, and yeast at 1.2%.

| Target dough | Flour | Water at 70% | Salt | What it fills |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 650 g | 375 g | 263 g | 7.5 g | 8.5x4.5 inch pan |
| 850 g | 491 g | 344 g | 9.8 g | 9x5 inch pan |
| 1,700 g | 982 g | 687 g | 19.6 g | Two 9x5 pans |
| 2,000 g | 1,155 g | 809 g | 23.1 g | A large round or a sheet |

Those 1,700 g figures are the ones worth taping inside a cabinet door. Two sandwich loaves, one mixing bowl, no arithmetic at 6 a.m.

## The Mistake That Turns 70% Into 233%

The percentage is measured against flour. Not against the dough, not against the water, not against the total on the scale.

Someone aiming for an 850 g loaf who takes 70% of 850 lands on 595 g of water, leaves 255 g for the flour, and mixes a batter at **233% hydration**. It will not shape. It will not even come together. The flour is the 100%, always, and the water is a fraction of it.

The second version of the same error is measuring both by volume. One cup of all-purpose flour weighs about 120 g and one cup of water weighs 240 g, so equal cups of each is a **200%** dough, not a 100% one. Flour and water only match at 1:1 when both are on a scale.

## Where the 350 Gram Answer Drifts

The arithmetic is exact. The flour is not.

**Scooped flour.** Four cups of bread flour at 127 g per cup is 508 g, so 350 g of water reads 68.9%. Pack the scoop to 132 g per cup and four cups is 528 g, which drops the same water to 66.3%. Weigh the flour once and this entire class of error disappears.

**A whole wheat blend.** Whole wheat absorbs 5 to 10 points more water than white flour before it feels the same. A 70% dough with 30% whole wheat handles like a 62% white dough, even though the math is identical.

**Flour that has been open for months.** Wheat flour picks up and loses moisture with the room. The difference is small, maybe 1 to 2 points, and it shows up as a dough that needs a splash at the end of mixing.

**Recipes that quote hydration to a decimal.** 70.0% on a home scale with 1 g resolution is honest to about half a point. Treat anything past that as decoration.

## Hold Back 25 Grams and Add It Later

Take 25 g out of the 350 and set it aside. Mix with 325 g, which is 65%, and the dough comes together faster and handles easier. Add the held-back water during the second fold, 10 g at a time, letting each addition absorb before the next.

The final hydration is still 70%. Only the order changed, and the order matters more than most formulas admit. A 65% mix followed by a hydration bump gives a stronger gluten network than dumping all 350 g in at once, because the flour hydrates in two stages instead of drowning.

Water temperature is the other lever at this stage. For a 76°F dough with 68°F flour in a 71°F kitchen, the water should be **89°F**: (3 × 76) − 68 − 71. Cold water out of the tap will push bulk fermentation from 3 hours to 5.
