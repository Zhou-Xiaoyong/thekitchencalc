Almost every recipe on the internet is written for four, six, or eight people. But real life rarely matches: tonight it's two, next week it's a potluck for twenty. Doing the math by hand — divide every ingredient by the original yield, then multiply by the target — is exactly the kind of fiddly arithmetic that leads to "⅔ of ¾ cup" and a ruined bake.

A **free recipe portion calculator** removes that step entirely. ## What a Portion Calculator Actually Does

Under the hood it's one line of math: `scaled amount = original amount × (target servings ÷ original servings)`. The value is in the execution — it handles the awkward fractions, keeps units consistent, and writes the result the way you actually measure.

| You have | You want | Scale factor | 2 cups flour becomes |
|----------|----------|--------------|----------------------|
| Serves 4 | Serves 2 | ×0.5 | 1 cup |
| Serves 6 | Serves 2 | ×0.33 | ⅔ cup |
| Serves 4 | Serves 10 | ×2.5 | 5 cups |
| Makes 12 | Makes 30 | ×2.5 | (per-item: 2.5×) |
| Serves 8 | Serves 3 | ×0.375 | ¾ cup |

The harder the factor, the more a calculator beats mental math. A "serves 8 → serves 3" factor of 0.375 turns 1 cup into ⅜ cup — a measurement most people can't produce by eye.

## When the Portion Factor Gets Tricky

A free calculator handles the math, but a few cases still need your judgment:

- **Eggs don't divide.** A factor that yields "1.5 eggs" means whisk one egg and use the right weight, or round to the nearest whole egg and nudge the liquid slightly. The calculator flags these; you decide.
- **Pan capacity isn't a portion problem.** Scaling a recipe to 20 servings doesn't change how full your 9×13 pan should be. Check the volume against your bakeware with our [Baking Pan Converter](/baking-pan-converter/) so a bigger batch doesn't overflow or bake raw in the center.
- **Cook time follows size, not servings.** More portions in one deep dish means more time; more portions split across two pans means about the same time. Trust doneness cues over a timer.
