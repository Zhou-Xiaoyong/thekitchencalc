## How to Read a Grams to Cups Conversion Chart

A chart taped inside a cabinet door says 1 cup of flour is 120 grams. Your scoop just put 138 grams on the scale, and the dough is already mixed.

That gap isn't the chart lying. It's the chart doing its job with an average. The 120 g figure is what a cup of all-purpose flour weighs when you spoon it into the cup and sweep it level. Dig the cup straight into the bag and you compact the flour by 10 to 15 percent without noticing. Same cup, same flour, different number.

Which means a conversion chart only works when you match the measuring method it was built on. Every number below comes from the spooned-and-leveled set the site's calculators use, so it lines up exactly with [the printable conversion chart](/conversion-chart/) if you keep one on the fridge.

Read down the 1-cup column and the whole problem shows up at once. A cup of rolled oats is 90 grams. A cup of honey is 340 grams. That's a 3.8-to-1 spread inside one kitchen, which is why the old shortcut of treating a cup as 240 g works for stock and milk and ruins everything else.

## Why Two Charts Give You Different Numbers

Search the same ingredient across three charts and you'll get three answers. Bread flour shows up as 127 g on one page and 130 g on another. Whole wheat swings between 120 and 128. None of them are typos.

Three things drive the disagreement:

- **Method.** Spooned and leveled is the reference. Scooped directly from the bag runs 10 to 15 percent heavier every time.
- **Milling.** Two brands of whole wheat flour hold different amounts of bran, and bran is light. A stone-ground flour can sit 8 g per cup below a supermarket brand.
- **Preparation.** A packed cup of brown sugar is 220 g. Loosely spooned and unpacked, the same cup is closer to 180 g, a 22 percent swing that changes cookie spread. Powdered sugar shifts the other way: 120 g unsifted, roughly 100 g after sifting.

The practical rule is dull but correct. Pick one chart and use only that chart for an entire recipe. Mixing a 130 g bread flour figure from one source with a 227 g butter figure from another is how a formula drifts 5 percent without anyone touching the method.

## Amounts the Chart Doesn't List

Most charts stop at 1 cup, ½ cup, ¼ cup, and 1 tbsp. Real recipes ask for ⅔ cup and 2⅓ cups. Since density doesn't change with the amount, you can multiply straight across the 1-cup figure.

- **⅔ cup bread flour:** 127 × 0.667 = 84.7, call it **85 g**
- **¾ cup granulated sugar:** 200 × 0.75 = **150 g**
- **2⅓ cups all-purpose flour:** 120 × 2.333 = **280 g**
- **⅛ cup cocoa:** 100 ÷ 8 = **12.5 g**

You can also walk there through tablespoons, since 16 tbsp make a cup. For ⅔ cup of bread flour that's 10 tbsp plus 2 tsp, and at 8 g per tablespoon you land on 84 g. One gram off the multiplication route. At that scale it does not matter, which is worth remembering the next time a chart and a calculator disagree by a rounding step.

The one place interpolation breaks is a lumpy ingredient. Chopped nuts and shredded cheese don't pack predictably, so a ⅔-cup figure derived from 120 g can be off by 15 g. Weigh those if the amount is large, and eyeball it if it's a garnish.

## Check Your Cup Before You Trust the Chart

Your measuring cup is probably fine. Your scooping is the variable. Here's a two-minute calibration that tells you which one you're dealing with.

1. Set a bowl on the scale and tare it to zero.
2. Spoon all-purpose flour into a dry 1-cup measure until it mounds above the rim.
3. Sweep the excess off with the flat back of a knife, not the curved side.
4. Weigh it. Repeat three times and average.

A spooned cup should read 118 to 124 g. If you average 132 g, your factor is 132 ÷ 120 = 1.10, so every flour number in the chart is 10 percent light for your hands. Either multiply as you go or, better, spend a week scooping more gently and recalibrate.

The test is worth repeating with brown sugar, because that's where packing style hides. Press the sugar hard into the cup and you'll hit 240 g instead of 220.

## Five Traps the Table Can't Warn You About

- **Honey versus oil.** Both pour, both look like a cup. Honey is 340 g per cup, oil is 218 g. Swap them by volume and you've added 122 g of sugar to the batter.
- **"1 cup brown sugar" without a packing note.** Assume packed for American baking recipes. Assume loose for anything translated from European weights, because the original was probably never in cups.
- **Shredded cheese by grate size.** A fine microplane grater produces 90 g per cup; the large holes on a box grater produce 113 g. Same block of cheddar.
- **Rice, uncooked versus cooked.** The 185 g figure is raw. A cup of cooked long-grain rice weighs about 158 g, and no chart will stop you from using the wrong row.
- **Cocoa powder, which compacts.** 100 g per cup spooned, closer to 125 g if the tin is low and you're scraping. Sift it and you're back under 100.

## When a Chart Stops Being Useful

Charts convert one ingredient at a time. They don't touch the thing that actually goes wrong when a recipe changes size: the ratios. Doubling a cake and adding the flour by two separate cup conversions doubles your rounding error along with the batter.
