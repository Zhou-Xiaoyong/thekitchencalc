## Why Convert a Recipe from Cups to Grams?

Cups are how most American home recipes are written, and they work fine — until you want the same result twice. A cup of flour depends on how you filled it, how humid the kitchen is, and whether the bag was freshly opened. Scooped flour runs 140 g; spooned and leveled, it runs 120 g. Same cup, same flour, 17% apart.

Grams remove that variable. When you convert a recipe from cups to grams, every future batch starts from the same number, and you can scale it up or down with a calculator instead of a stack of measuring cups.

The conversion itself is not hard. It is one multiplication per ingredient. The part people get wrong is the density — how many grams actually live in one cup of that specific ingredient.

## The Rule That Makes Every Conversion Work

**One density per ingredient. Never one density for the recipe.**

A cup measures volume (space), and grams measure mass (stuff). The only thing that bridges them is how densely the ingredient packs. Water is 240 g per cup. All-purpose flour is 120. Honey is 340. Cocoa powder is about 85. There is no universal number, no matter how many conversion articles tell you "1 cup = 240 g."

So the workflow for any recipe is:

1. **List every ingredient** with its cup amount.
2. **Look up the cup weight** for that exact ingredient.
3. **Multiply** the cup amount by the cup weight.
4. **Round to a practical increment** — 1 g for small amounts, 5 g for large ones.
5. **Write the new recipe card in grams only**, so you never mix methods mid-bake.

| Ingredient | 1 cup | ½ cup | ⅓ cup | ¼ cup |
|------------|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| All-purpose flour | 120 g | 60 g | 40 g | 30 g |
| Bread flour | 130 g | 65 g | 43 g | 33 g |
| Cake flour | 114 g | 57 g | 38 g | 29 g |
| Granulated sugar | 200 g | 100 g | 67 g | 50 g |
| Brown sugar (packed) | 220 g | 110 g | 73 g | 55 g |
| Powdered sugar | 120 g | 60 g | 40 g | 30 g |
| Butter | 227 g | 113 g | 76 g | 57 g |
| Vegetable oil | 218 g | 109 g | 73 g | 55 g |
| Milk | 245 g | 122 g | 82 g | 61 g |
| Water | 240 g | 120 g | 80 g | 60 g |
| Honey | 340 g | 170 g | 113 g | 85 g |
| Cocoa powder | 85 g | 43 g | 28 g | 21 g |
| Rolled oats | 90 g | 45 g | 30 g | 23 g |
| Chocolate chips | 170 g | 85 g | 57 g | 43 g |
| Chopped nuts | 120 g | 60 g | 40 g | 30 g |

Note how far apart honey (340 g) and cocoa (85 g) sit — four times the weight for the same volume. Anyone who converted both at 240 g has already wrecked the recipe. ## Worked Example: A Banana Bread in Grams

Take a typical American banana bread written in volume:

- ½ cup butter
- ¾ cup granulated sugar
- 2 large eggs
- 1½ cups all-purpose flour
- 1 teaspoon baking soda
- ½ teaspoon salt
- 3 ripe bananas

Converted to grams:

1. Butter: ½ × 227 = **113 g**
2. Sugar: ¾ × 200 = **150 g**
3. Eggs: 2 large, weigh whole — roughly **100 g** shelled. (Eggs are the one ingredient worth weighing as a group and writing as "100 g, about 2 eggs.")
4. Flour: 1½ × 120 = **180 g**
5. Baking soda: 1 tsp ≈ **5 g**
6. Salt: ½ tsp ≈ **3 g**
7. Bananas: 3 medium, mashed — roughly **345 g**

That is now a recipe you can double without thinking, halve without guessing, and hand to anyone with a scale anywhere in the world. The ratios of flour to sugar (180:150) and flour to fat (180:113) stay visible, which is how you spot a mis-scaled batch before it hits the oven.

## Where Volume-to-Weight Conversion Goes Wrong

Most failed conversions come from four mistakes, and each has a simple guard:

- **Packing brown sugar.** Brown sugar is the one ingredient you press into the cup. If you convert using the loose 180 g figure instead of the packed 220 g figure, you come up 40 g short on sugar.
- **Scooping flour.** The 120 g cup assumes spoon-and-level. Convert a recipe using 120 g and then measure the original by scooping, and your "before" and "after" batches are not the same recipe.
- **Treating liquids as interchangeable.** Water is 240, milk is 245, oil is 218. Close enough for soup, not for a cake where fat is doing structural work.
- **Converting sifted vs unsifted flour.** "1 cup sifted flour" is about 100 g; "1 cup flour, sifted" is 120 g that then gets sifted. Different amounts, and older recipes assume you know it.

## Convert and Scale in the Same Pass

If you are changing servings at the same time, do the math in this order: **convert to grams first, then multiply by the scaling factor.** Converting after scaling means multiplying fractions of cups, which is where tablespoons go missing.

For example, a 12-serving recipe at 1½ cups flour becomes 180 g. Scaling to 20 servings is 180 × (20 ÷ 12) = **300 g**. Try that with cups and you get 2.5 cups, then have to figure out what 2.5 cups means on a measuring cup with ⅓ and ¾ markings. ## Common Conversions at a Glance

- 1 cup all-purpose flour = **120 g**
- 1 cup granulated sugar = **200 g**
- 1 cup packed brown sugar = **220 g**
- 1 cup butter = **227 g** (2 sticks)
- 1 cup liquid = **218–245 g** depending on the liquid
- 1 cup honey = **340 g**

Keep these six numbers in your head and you can convert most American recipes on the spot. Anything outside that list — semolina, almond flour, coconut flour — is worth a quick lookup rather than a guess.
