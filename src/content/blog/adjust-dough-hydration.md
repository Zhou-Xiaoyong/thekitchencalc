## One Teaspoon of Water Is One Point

Twenty minutes into the rest, the dough is still spreading flat across the bowl. Your finger pulls a strand an inch long before it lets go. You were aiming for 65% and the bowl in front of you is closer to 70%.

The correction is smaller than your instinct says. On 500 g of flour, **one level teaspoon of water is one hydration point**. Five grams, one percent, done.

The same rule runs backward with a different exchange rate. One tablespoon of bread flour, about 8 g on our [conversion chart](/conversion-chart/), pulls a 500 g batch down roughly one point. Water is the fast knob. Flour is the slow one, and turning it costs you something else every time.

## Two Knobs, Two Exchange Rates

Start from a plain lean batch: 500 g bread flour, 350 g water, 10 g salt, 6 g active dry yeast. That is 70.0% and 866 g of dough. Here is what a single spoon does to it.

| Change | New hydration | What else moves |
|---|---|---|
| +1 tsp water (5 g) | 71.0% | Nothing measurable; dough 871 g |
| +2 tsp water (10 g) | 72.0% | Calculator label flips Medium to High |
| +1 tbsp + 1 tsp water (20 g) | 74.0% | Salt in the water phase drops 2.86% to 2.70% |
| +1 tbsp flour (8 g) | 68.9% | Salt falls to 1.97% of flour |
| +2 tbsp flour (16 g) | 67.8% | Salt 1.94%, yeast 1.16% |

Water only moves the water number. Flour moves the water number and every other percentage in the formula at once, because flour is the 100% everything else is measured against. Add 16 g of flour and your 2% salt is suddenly a 1.94% dough with slightly less yeast activity than the card promised. It will still bake. It will just ferment on a schedule you did not plan.

That 72.0% row is the interesting one. 68% and 72% feel like neighbors, and our [dough hydration calculator](/dough-hydration-calculator/) files them in different bands, Medium under 72 and High above it. The label describes handling, not quality.

## Read the Dough Before You Touch It

Do not judge at minute two. Flour needs 15 to 20 minutes to finish absorbing, and a mix that feels tight coming off the hook often turns out correct by the time you come back. Judge at 20 minutes, then again at the end of bulk.

| Stage | Looks too wet | Looks too dry | What you can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 min after mixing | Finger pulls a strand, dough slumps to the bowl edge | Surface cracks, tears instead of stretching | Water only, 5 g at a time |
| End of bulk, doubled | No dome, bubbles on top, dough slides when tilted | Tight and small, tears at the fold | 5 g water under the next fold |
| Preshape | Spreads past 6 inches in 10 minutes | Springs back to a 4-inch disc | Nothing. Change the shape |
| After shaping | Sticks to the banneton liner | Seam splits before it hits the oven | Nothing. Bake it |

The last two rows are the honest ones. Once the dough has been preshaped, its structure is built around the water it has, and more water does not dissolve into it, it sits on the surface and wrecks the skin you just made.

## Why Late Water Beats Late Flour

Five grams of water disappears into a dough that is already hydrated. Thirty seconds of folding and you cannot find it. Twenty grams of flour dropped into a wet dough does the opposite: it clumps, it needs a 15 minute rest to absorb, and until it does you cannot tell whether you fixed anything.

There is a second cost. Every gram of flour you add late dilutes the salt and yeast you measured at the start, and dilution shows up hours later as a loaf that proofed slower than the last one with no obvious reason why. Water has no such side effect. Add water, and the only number that changes is the one you were trying to change.

Salt perception tracks the water phase, not the total dough. Ten grams of salt in 350 g of water is a 2.86% brine inside the dough; 20 g more water drops it to 2.70%. That is why a dough you corrected upward can taste flat even though you never touched the salt.

## The Three-Point Budget

Fifteen grams of water on a 500 g batch. That is the honest size of a mid-mix correction. Past three points, you are no longer adjusting the dough you planned, you are building a different one, and the gluten network has already formed around the water level it had.

When the dough is more than three points off, stop correcting and change the bread:

- **Too wet to shape at 78% or above.** Oil a 13x18 half sheet, spread the dough to the corners with wet fingers, dimple it, and bake at 450°F for 20 to 25 minutes. That is focaccia, and it is better than a loaf that collapsed.
- **Too stiff to open at 60% or below.** Roll it into a log instead of a boule, proof seam down in a 9x5 pan, and call it sandwich bread. Stiff dough forgives a pan. It does not forgive a banneton.
- **Somewhere in between with no time left.** Shape it smaller than you planned. A 700 g boule from an 866 g batch bakes fine. The remainder becomes two rolls.

## Hold Back a Quarter of the Water

The easiest way to adjust hydration is to leave yourself something to adjust with. Take 25 g out of the 350 g and set it aside. Mix at 325 g, which is 65%, and let the dough come together for 20 minutes.

Then add the held-back water in two pours, one at the second fold and one at the third, 10 g at a time, waiting until each addition disappears before the next. The final hydration is still 70%. The order changed, and the order is the part most formulas skip. A dough mixed at 65% and brought up builds a tighter gluten network than one that got all 350 g at once, because the flour hydrates in stages rather than swimming.

Held-back water also doubles as your correction budget. If the dough looks right at 65% and the kitchen is warm, keep the 25 g in the cup and bake at 65%. Nothing is wasted.

Water temperature belongs in this step, not at the top. For a 75°F dough with 70°F room temperature and 73°F flour, the water should be **82°F**: (3 × 75) − 70 − 73. Cold tap water at 58°F will push a 3 hour bulk to nearly 5.

## Temperature Is an Adjustment You Didn't Count

A dough mixed at 78°F handles looser than the same formula at 70°F. Warm gluten stretches further before it tears, so the 70% dough in an August kitchen often behaves like the 72% version you baked in February. If your kitchen runs hot, drop the formula two points and let the temperature do the rest.

The fermentation side is the bigger effect. Bulk fermentation at 78°F finishes 30 to 45 minutes sooner than at 70°F, which means the hydration you adjusted for a 4 hour rise is now sitting in a dough that peaked at 3:15. Check at 75% of your usual time the first bake in a new season.

## Write Down What You Did

A correction you cannot repeat is a one-time save. Four lines in a notebook turns it into a formula.

| Bake | Flour | Water poured | Added later | Bench note | Next bake |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 12 | 500 g bread flour | 325 g | +10 g at fold 2 | Still slack at preshape, spread to 7 in | Start at 66% |
| Oct 19 | 500 g bread flour | 330 g | 0 | Cleared the bowl at 20 min | Keep 66% |

Thirty seconds of writing beats an hour of guessing next Saturday. If you bake two loaves at once, log them separately, because the [recipe servings calculator](/recipe-servings-calculator/) will scale the numbers but it will not tell you that the second loaf went into a hotter oven.

Flour changes more than people expect. A new bag or a new brand can shift absorption by five points before you touch the dough. If a formula that worked in September is slack in January, the flour is the first suspect, not your folding.
