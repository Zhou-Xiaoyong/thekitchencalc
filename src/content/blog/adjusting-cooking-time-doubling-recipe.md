## Doubling a Recipe Changes the Clock

Here's the mistake almost everyone makes once: they double a recipe, double the cook time, and end up with either a burnt edge or a cold center. The truth about **adjusting cooking time when doubling a recipe** is that time usually goes *up* — but nowhere near double, and the rules depend entirely on what you're cooking.

The reason is physics. Heat moves from the outside in. When you double a pot of chili, you haven't doubled the surface area where heat enters — you've doubled the volume that heat has to travel through. So the center takes longer to come up to temperature, even though the outside cooks at the same rate.

## The Core Principle: Surface Area vs Volume

A shallow 9×13 pan of casserole has lots of surface exposed to hot air. Pour that same recipe into a deeper pan and the middle is buried — it needs more time. This single idea explains every rule below:

- **More volume, same surface** → longer cook time.
- **Same volume split across more pans** → roughly the original time, in parallel.
- **Oven temperature** → stays the same. Doubling does not mean lowering the heat; it means extending the duration.

## Cooking Time Rules by Dish Type

Use this table as your starting point, then verify with a thermometer or a fork test rather than the clock.

| Dish | Time change when doubled | What's happening |
|------|--------------------------|------------------|
| Soups & stews | +25–50% to reach a simmer; similar once boiling | Bigger pot, much longer heat-up |
| Casserole, one deeper pan | +15–25% | More depth to heat through |
| Casserole, two pans | Same per pan | Bake both racks, rotate halfway |
| Roast (whole piece) | Almost none | Temp constant; time set by weight |
| Cookies / sheet bakes | Same | Multiple sheets, rotate racks |
| Braises | +20–40% | More mass to come to temp |
| Rice / grains | +10–20% | Deeper layer, slower absorption |

Two cautions worth memorizing: a doubled soup can take 30–60 minutes just to reach a simmer in a large pot, and a doubled casserole in one pan often needs 15–25% more time but risks a dry top — cover it for the first part, then uncover to brown.

## Why Temperature Stays the Same

Doubling the *amount* doesn't change the *temperature* food needs to be done. Chicken is still safe at 165°F whether the batch is single or double. What changes is how long the center takes to get there. If a doubled dish isn't done, add time in 10–15 minute increments and check — don't crank the heat, or you'll burn the outside first.

## A Worked Example: Double a Pot of Chili

Original (serves 4): 1 lb ground beef, 1 can tomatoes, 1 cup beans, 2 cups broth; simmer 30 minutes.

Doubled (serves 8): 2 lb beef, 2 cans tomatoes, 2 cups beans, 4 cups broth.

- Brown the beef in two batches so it sears instead of steams.
- Expect 30–45 minutes just to reach a simmer in the larger pot.
- Once simmering, cook 35–45 minutes (slightly longer than the original 30) for flavors to meld.
- Total active + cook time runs about 1.6x the original, not 2x — the heat-up dominates.

Check doneness by taste and texture, not by the timer, and hold hot food at 140°F or above if it waits for serving.
