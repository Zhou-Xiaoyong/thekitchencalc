## A Batch Weighs 1,263 Grams and the Flour Is 270 of Them

Weigh the dough from the back of the chocolate chip bag and the bowl holds 1,263 g: 2¼ cups of flour, a cup of butter, two sugars, two eggs, a teaspoon each of soda and salt, and two cups of chips. The flour in that total is 270 g.

Twenty-one percent.

Bread formulas sit at 57% to 60% flour by weight, so flour earns the 100% line and everything else is a fraction of it. Cookie dough keeps the arithmetic and loses the argument. Flour is still the base. It is no longer the dough.

## Flour Stays at 100 Because the Math Needs a Base

Baker's percentage is one rule: every ingredient weight divided by the total flour weight, times 100. Flour is always 100 because it is the divisor, not because it dominates the bowl.

A 100% line that represents a fifth of the mass cannot describe texture by itself. What it can do is resize a batch in one multiplication.

## The Whole Formula, Converted

Using our [conversion chart](/conversion-chart/) for the cup weights, this is the standard batch as percentages.

| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour | 270 g | 100% |
| Butter, softened | 227 g | 84.1% |
| Granulated sugar | 150 g | 55.6% |
| Brown sugar, packed | 165 g | 61.1% |
| Eggs, 2 large | 100 g | 37.0% |
| Baking soda | 4.6 g | 1.7% |
| Fine salt | 6 g | 2.2% |
| Chocolate chips | 340 g | 125.9% |
| **Total** | **1,263 g** | **467.6%** |

The chips alone are worth more than the flour.

Those percentages also check the batch: 270 × 4.676 = 1,263 g. Change the flour to 400 g and the dough becomes 1,870 g with no other arithmetic.

## Four Cookies, Same 100 Percent, Four Different Doughs

The flour line is identical in all four rows. Everything that makes them different sits in the other columns.

| Cookie | Butter | Sugar | Egg | Bakes at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shortbread | 67% | 33% | 0% | 325°F, 20 min |
| Cut-out sugar cookie | 57% | 50% | 17% | 375°F, 8 min |
| Chocolate chip | 84% | 117% | 37% | 350°F, 10 min |
| Oatmeal raisin | 85% | 100% | 25% | 350°F, 11 min |

Shortbread at 33% holds a shape and snaps. The chocolate chip dough at 117% slumps into a dome and cracks on top. Sugar liquefies in the oven, and the more of it there is per gram of flour, the further the edge travels before the structure sets.

The butter column works the same way. Fat coats flour proteins and shortens gluten, so more butter means a more tender crumb and also a wider spread, because butter releases its water as steam at 212°F and the fat itself is liquid by 95°F. Both effects push outward.

## Real Water Is 41 Percent, and the Label Cannot Help

Add up what is actually water. Two large eggs carry about 76 g. A cup of butter is roughly 16% water, so another 36 g. Call it 112 g against 270 g of flour.

**Forty-one percent.** Every bread formula on this site starts at 58 and climbs. Cookies live twenty points below the driest loaf and still hold together, because sugar syrup and fat are doing the binding that gluten does in bread.

Paste 270 and 112 into the [Dough Hydration Calculator](/dough-hydration-calculator/) and it reports "Very Low Hydration," the band it reserves for pasta and hard pretzels. The label is not wrong about the arithmetic. It is answering a question cookies do not ask. Keep the calculator for the loaves and use the percentage columns for the sweets.

## One Spoon Is Worth Two in Bread

Sensitivity is the practical difference. At 270 g of flour, one point of percentage is 2.7 g.

| Addition | Weight | Points added |
|---|---|---|
| 1 tsp water | 5 g | 1.9 |
| 1 tbsp water | 15 g | 5.6 |
| 1 tbsp butter | 14 g | 5.2 |
| 1 tbsp granulated sugar | 12.5 g | 4.6 |
| 1 tbsp flour | 7.5 g | drops every other line 2.7% |

On a 500 g bread formula a teaspoon of water is one point. Here it is nearly two, because the divisor is smaller. That is why a cookie recipe punishes an extra splash and a bread recipe shrugs it off.

The flour row is the odd one. Adding flour does not raise a percentage, it lowers all of them at once. A single extra tablespoon takes butter from 84.1% to 81.9% and sugar from 117% to 114%, which is enough to turn a chewy cookie crisp.

## Oats and Cocoa Never Reach the 100 Line

This is where the system quietly fails. An oatmeal cookie at 200 g flour and 150 g oats reads 75% on the oats. That sounds harmless. Oats absorb water the way flour does, so the dough behaves like one with 350 g of flour in it while the percentage column says 200.

Cocoa powder is worse. Most cards leave it outside the flour line, yet it drinks liquid like flour, so a double-chocolate dough at a cheerful 90% butter feels stiff at the scoop.

Two fixes:

- Put every water-absorbing powder inside the 100% line. Flour plus oats plus cocoa is the divisor.
- Leave chips, nuts, and dried fruit outside it. They add weight and no absorption, which is exactly how the 125.9% chip line should read.

## Butter at 65°F Beats Any Percentage

No column in the table records butter temperature, and it moves the bake more than five points of sugar would.

Butter at 65°F creams with sugar into a pale, air-filled mass, and that trapped air expands in the oven. Butter at 75°F smears instead of creaming, the air never gets in, and the cookies flatten into lace. Straight from the fridge at 38°F, it stays in lumps and the cookies bake unevenly with hard pockets.

Pull the butter 45 minutes before you start in a 70°F kitchen. If you forgot, cut it into half-inch cubes and give it 15 minutes. The microwave is a losing bet: 10 seconds too long and the edges melt while the center is still hard.

## Twenty-Four Hours in the Fridge Changes the Bake, Not the Numbers

Chill the dough and not one percentage moves. The dough does.

At 41% water there is little free liquid, and flour needs hours to hydrate fully. A day in the fridge gives it that time, which is why chilled dough handles drier and bakes with a tighter grain.

- **Spread.** The same 42 g scoop bakes about a quarter inch narrower after a 24-hour chill, because the butter starts cold and has to melt before the edge can move.
- **Time.** Add 1 to 2 minutes. A chilled scoop at 350°F wants 11 to 13 minutes where the fresh dough wanted 9 to 11.
- **Color.** The crust darkens earlier, since the sugars have had a day to break down. Check at 9 minutes the first time.

Past 72 hours the dough starts to gray and taste of the fridge. Two days is the sweet spot, and one is enough if you are baking on a weeknight.

## Scaling to 60 Cookies

Portions are where percentages earn their place. Each cookie is its own unit, so the batch scales linearly, unlike a cake where pan depth changes the bake.

A 1,263 g batch divided into 42 g scoops is 30 cookies. Sixty cookies is 2,520 g of dough, which is 540 g of flour and every other line doubled:

| For 60 cookies | Weight |
|---|---|
| Flour | 540 g |
| Butter | 454 g |
| Granulated sugar | 300 g |
| Brown sugar | 330 g |
| Eggs | 200 g |
| Baking soda | 9.2 g |
| Salt | 12 g |
| Chips | 680 g |

Count your cookies once. If 1,263 g gives you 34 instead of 30, each one is 37 g and needs about a minute less. The percentage did not change. Your scoop did.
