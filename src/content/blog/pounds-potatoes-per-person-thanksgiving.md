## Start With Six

Twelve people, six pounds of potatoes. That is half a pound of raw potato per person, and it is the number our [holiday meal planner](/holiday-meal-planner/) multiplies against your guest count when you check the mashed potatoes box.

Six pounds is also not what most hosts buy. They buy the five-pound bag, because that is the bag, and it lands ten cups of mash on the table for twelve people.

## Where the Half Pound Comes From

USDA nutrient data puts a one-cup serving of mashed potatoes at about 210 grams, or 7.4 ounces. Roughly 85 percent of that weight is potato and the rest is milk or cream, which leaves **6.3 ounces of actual tuber per serving**, or 0.39 pounds.

So the familiar half-pound rule is not arbitrary. It is the government serving plus a 27 percent cushion. That cushion is the whole point at Thanksgiving, where a cup of potatoes is a modest scoop and the gravy boat makes people take a second pass.

Three numbers beat one:

| Your crowd | Raw potato per person | What it looks like on the plate |
|---|---|---|
| Light eaters, big sides elsewhere | ⅓ lb | A ¾-cup scoop |
| A normal Thanksgiving table | ½ lb | One cup, no seconds |
| Big eaters, or you want Friday leftovers | ¾ lb | One cup plus a real second helping |

## Six Pounds for Twelve, and What It Fills

One pound of raw potatoes yields about two cups of finished mash, because the butter and cream add back roughly what the peel and steam took away. That ratio runs the whole table.

| Guests | Raw potatoes | Finished mash | Smallest pot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 3 lb | 6 cups | 2 qt |
| 8 | 4 lb | 8 cups | 3 qt |
| 10 | 5 lb | 10 cups | 3 qt |
| 12 | 6 lb | 12 cups | 4 qt |
| 16 | 8 lb | 16 cups | 5 qt |
| 20 | 10 lb | 20 cups | 6 qt |

The last column is where twelve people stop being a math problem and start being a dish problem. Twelve cups is three quarts exactly, so a four-quart pot or slow cooker holds it with enough headroom to stir. Ladled into a 9x13 instead, twelve cups sits an inch and a half deep, which is why the potatoes at the end of the buffet are cold. Two smaller bowls beat one wide one.

## Peel Loss Eats the Margin

A vegetable peeler takes 10 to 15 percent of the weight. A paring knife takes more if you chase the eyes.

| Stage of one pound | What you have |
|---|---|
| Whole, unpeeled | 16 oz, about three medium potatoes |
| After peeling | 13.5 to 14.5 oz |
| Boiled, mashed, no dairy | about 1¾ cups |
| With butter and cream | about 2 cups |

A recipe that calls for **six pounds of peeled potatoes** wants seven pounds from the store. Buy six and you have 5.2 pounds in the pot, which is 10½ cups, which feeds ten.

Weigh before peeling. Bag weight and recipe weight are different units even though both say pounds.

## The Bag Decides More Than the Math

| Bag | Potato count | Feeds, at ½ lb each |
|---|---|---|
| 5 lb | 12 to 15 medium | 10 people |
| 8 lb | 20 to 24 medium | 16 people |
| 10 lb | 25 to 30 medium | 20 people |

Nobody sells a six-pound bag of russets, so twelve guests means a five plus a two, or a ten with four pounds left over. Take the ten. Raw potatoes keep for weeks in a cool dark cupboard and become Thursday's home fries. Mashed potatoes do not keep, so an overbuy before cooking costs nothing while an underbuy costs the meal.

Medium here means five to six ounces. A "large" potato in the produce aisle runs eight to ten ounces, so counting instead of weighing is how a six-pound plan turns into eight.

## Pot Size Is What Actually Goes Wrong

Diced raw potato takes up about two and a half cups per pound, and the water needs to cover it by an inch.

| Raw potatoes | Dice volume | Pot |
|---|---|---|
| 3 lb | 7½ cups | 4 qt |
| 5 lb | 12½ cups | 6 qt |
| 6 lb | 15 cups | 8 qt |
| 10 lb | 25 cups | 12 qt |

A six-quart pot holds five pounds comfortably and six pounds badly. The bad version is not a disaster, it is a splatter: the water barely covers the top layer, the pot comes back to a boil slowly, and the potatoes at the rim sit above the waterline and stay firm while the ones underneath go to mush. If six pounds must go into a six-quart pot, cut the chunks smaller and top up the water as they settle.

## Dairy Scales With the Potatoes

Butter and cream follow the weight of the potatoes, not the number of guests. This is the ratio our [make-ahead sides guide](/blog/make-ahead-thanksgiving-sides/) uses for a five-pound batch.

| Raw potatoes | Butter | Cream or half-and-half | Sour cream | Fine salt |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 lb | 5 tbsp | ¾ cup | ¼ cup | 1 tsp |
| 5 lb | 8 tbsp (1 stick) | 1 cup | ½ cup | 1½ tsp |
| 6 lb | 10 tbsp | 1¼ cups | ½ cup | 2 tsp |
| 10 lb | 16 tbsp (2 sticks) | 2 cups | ¾ cup | 1 tbsp |

Warm the dairy first. Cold cream drops the pot's temperature and makes the mash seize, so you mash longer to fix it, and that is what actually makes potatoes gluey. Heat it until hot to the touch, not boiling.

Add the fat first, mash, then add the liquid a third at a time. A ten-pound batch that took two cups will not take three just because you poured.

## Fifteen Minutes, and Not One Minute at a Rolling Boil

Cut peeled potatoes into **1½-inch chunks**, cover with cold water by an inch, and salt the water with a teaspoon per pound. Starting cold brings the outside and the inside up together, so the cubes finish at the same time instead of the edges collapsing while the center is still hard.

Bring to a boil, then drop to a bare simmer. A hard boil throws the pieces against each other, ruptures the cells, and releases starch into the water. That starch is what turns mash to paste.

Fifteen to twenty minutes, until a paring knife slides through the largest chunk with no resistance. Drain immediately and return the pot to low heat for thirty seconds to drive off the surface water, because wet potatoes take more dairy and taste thinner.

Hold finished mash above 140°F. The [food safety temperature guide](/food-safety-temperature-guide/) puts the danger zone's floor there, and a bowl of mash on a buffet crosses it in about forty minutes.

## Who Actually Eats Them

The guest count on your list is not the guest count at the table, and potatoes show that gap most.

- **Children under ten count as half.** They take a spoonful, push it around, and ask about pie.
- **One or two adults will skip them.** Low-carb, or saving room for stuffing.
- **Seconds are the norm, not the exception.** Mashed potatoes are the delivery system for gravy, and gravy is why the second scoop happens.
- **If there are two starches on the table**, mashed potatoes and sweet potato casserole both, expect each to move at about 70 percent of its usual rate.

A table of twelve with four children and two dieters still eats about eleven adult portions, because the seven who do take more than one scoop. That is the argument for the ¾-pound column.

## FAQ

### How many pounds of potatoes for 12 people?

Six pounds of raw potatoes, about twelve cups of mash, or one cup each. Buy eight if you want leftovers. Peeling costs 10 to 15 percent, so six pounds weighed whole lands nearer ten cups than twelve.

### Do I weigh potatoes before or after peeling?

Before, always, unless the recipe says "peeled weight" out loud. Peeling costs 10 to 15 percent, so six pounds of peeled potatoes means buying seven. A scale on the counter settles it faster than counting, because a large russet weighs twice what a medium one does.

### Can I boil the potatoes the day before Thanksgiving?

Yes, and it is the least risky make-ahead move on the menu. Boil, drain, rice into a buttered dish, cover, and refrigerate up to two days. Reheat covered at 325°F for 30 to 35 minutes with a splash of cream, or hold in a slow cooker on low for two hours.
