## Doubling Is Simple. Re-Measuring Is Not.

Multiplying a recipe by two is arithmetic anyone can do in their head. The actual difficulty is what comes next: turning "⅔ cup × 2" into something you can physically scoop, or figuring out what to do with 1½ eggs and 3 half-teaspoons of five different spices.

Knowing how to convert recipe measurements when doubling is really about three skills — collapsing fractions into larger units, switching to weight when volume gets messy, and recognizing the handful of ingredients that shouldn't be doubled at all.

## The Conversions You Need Memorized

Almost every doubling headache is solved by these six equivalencies:

| Unit | Equivalent |
|------|-----------|
| 3 teaspoons | 1 tablespoon |
| 4 tablespoons | ¼ cup |
| 8 tablespoons | ½ cup |
| 16 tablespoons | 1 cup |
| 2 cups | 1 pint (16 fl oz) |
| 4 cups | 1 quart (32 fl oz) |

With those in hand, any awkward doubled amount can be simplified. Doubling ⅜ cup gives ¾ cup. Doubling 1½ teaspoons gives 1 tablespoon. Doubling 5 tablespoons gives 10 tablespoons — which is ½ cup plus 2 tablespoons, far easier to measure than counting ten scoops.

## Step 1: Double, Then Simplify Upward

The workflow is always the same: multiply by 2, then convert the result into the largest practical unit. Here's the full doubling reference:

| Original | Doubled | Simplify To |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| ⅛ tsp | ¼ tsp | ¼ tsp |
| ¼ tsp | ½ tsp | ½ tsp |
| ¾ tsp | 1½ tsp | ½ tbsp |
| 1 tsp | 2 tsp | 2 tsp |
| 1½ tsp | 3 tsp | 1 tbsp |
| 2 tsp | 4 tsp | 1 tbsp + 1 tsp |
| 1 tbsp | 2 tbsp | ⅛ cup |
| 2 tbsp | 4 tbsp | ¼ cup |
| 3 tbsp | 6 tbsp | ¼ cup + 2 tbsp |
| ¼ cup | ½ cup | ½ cup |
| ⅓ cup | ⅔ cup | ⅔ cup |
| ⅜ cup | ¾ cup | ¾ cup |
| ½ cup | 1 cup | 1 cup |
| ⅔ cup | 1⅓ cups | 1⅓ cups |
| ¾ cup | 1½ cups | 1½ cups |
| 1 cup | 2 cups | 1 pint |
| 1¼ cups | 2½ cups | 2½ cups |

The pattern to notice: **doubling usually makes fractions simpler, not harder.** Thirds become two-thirds, eighths become quarters. It's the opposite of halving, where everything gets uglier.

## Step 2: Switch to Grams When Volume Gets Silly

Some doubled amounts have no clean volume equivalent. Doubling 1⅓ cups gives 2⅔ cups — measurable, but three separate scoops with three chances to over-pack. Doubling ⅝ teaspoon gives 1¼ teaspoons, which most spoon sets can't hit accurately.

This is where weight wins. Common baking densities per US cup:

| Ingredient | Grams per cup |
|-----------|---------------|
| All-purpose flour (spooned & leveled) | 120 g |
| Bread flour | 127 g |
| Granulated sugar | 200 g |
| Brown sugar (packed) | 220 g |
| Powdered sugar | 120 g |
| Butter | 227 g |
| Milk | 240 g |
| Water | 237 g |
| Rolled oats | 90 g |
| Cocoa powder | 85 g |

Doubling 1⅓ cups of flour becomes "320 g" — one number, one measurement, zero error. **Rule of thumb:** if a doubled measurement requires more than two scoops of the same cup, weigh it instead.

## How Do You Double 1½ Eggs or an Odd Egg Count?

Eggs are the one ingredient that resists doubling cleanly when the original recipe uses odd numbers or halves.

- **Recipe calls for 3 eggs → doubled = 6.** No problem.
- **Recipe calls for 1½ eggs → doubled = 3.** Also no problem, and easier than the original.
- **Recipe calls for 2 eggs + 1 yolk → doubled = 4 eggs + 2 yolks.** Straightforward, just don't forget the yolks.
- **Recipe calls for "1 egg, beaten, half reserved for wash" → weigh it.** A large egg is about 50 g out of the shell; measure by weight rather than guessing.

If you ever need a fraction of an egg, beat one whole egg and weigh out the proportion: 25 g is half, 33 g is two-thirds.

## Step 3: The Ingredients That Should Not Be Doubled

Converting measurements correctly still won't save a dish if you double the wrong things. These scale at roughly **1.5x**, not 2x:

- **Salt** — taste and adjust at the end rather than committing up front
- **Chili flakes, cayenne, black pepper** — heat compounds in larger volumes
- **Baking soda and baking powder** — over-leavened batter rises then collapses; 1.75x is a safer ceiling
- **Yeast** — 1.5x ferments a doubled dough on nearly the same timeline
- **Vanilla and other extracts** — concentrated flavors get harsh quickly
- **Wine, spirits, vinegar** — larger volumes evaporate proportionally less

Everything else — flour, sugar, dairy, stock, meat, vegetables, garlic, fresh herbs — takes the full 2x without complaint.

## Step 4: Re-Check Equipment Before You Start

A doubled recipe needs roughly double the space, and converted measurements are useless if the batter overflows.

- **Baking:** doubling an 8×8-inch pan (64 sq in) means you need about 128 sq in — a 9×13 (117 sq in) is close enough for most bakes, or use two 8×8s for identical timing. Our [Baking Pan Converter](/baking-pan-converter/) does the area math and suggests equivalents.
- **Stovetop:** keep the pot at two-thirds full or less after doubling.
- **Mixing bowls:** doubled batter needs a bowl with headroom, not a bowl that's technically large enough.

## Does Cooking Time Double Too?

No — and this is the mistake that ruins the most doubled dinners.

- Two identical pans in one oven: **same time**, rotate halfway through
- One deeper pan: **add 15–30%** and drop the temperature by 25°F (15°C)
- Soups and braises: **same simmer time**, just longer to come up to heat
- Two roasts: timing follows the size of *each* piece, not the combined weight

Rely on internal temperature and doneness cues instead of the clock whenever you've changed vessel depth.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Measuring ten tablespoons instead of converting to ½ cup + 2 tbsp
- Doubling salt, spice, and leaveners at a flat 2x
- Using cups for doubled amounts that need three or more scoops
- Keeping the original pan for a doubled batter
- Doubling the bake time "to be safe"
