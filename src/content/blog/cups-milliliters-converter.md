## Cups to Milliliters: The Short Answer

**1 US cup = 236.6 mL.** That is the number to remember for American recipes. But "cup" is not an international unit, and three different values are in daily use around the world:

- **US customary cup = 236.6 mL** — every American cookbook and recipe site.
- **US legal cup = 240 mL** — what nutrition labels and food packaging use.
- **Metric cup = 250 mL** — Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and most of Europe.
- **UK imperial cup = 284.1 mL** — older British cookbooks, pre-metrication.

If you convert a 250 mL metric cup as though it were a US cup, you are short by about 6%. On a single cup of stock that is nothing. Across a six-cup batch of batter, that is a third of a cup missing — enough to change crumb, rise, and bake time.

For everyday cooking, rounding 236.6 to 237 mL is fine. For baking where hydration matters — bread, laminated dough, macarons — the difference between 236 and 240 mL is worth respecting, because a 1.4% hydration swing is audible in the crumb.

## Which Cup Does Your Recipe Use?

Four quick signals decide it:

- **The recipe is from a US site or cookbook** → 236.6 mL. Flours and sugars are usually given in cups, and the author is almost certainly scooping US cups.
- **The recipe lists grams or millilitres first** → you are looking at a metric or Australian source. Assume 250 mL when it says "cup," or just use the gram figure and skip cups entirely.
- **The recipe mentions "gas mark" or was published in the UK before the 1970s** → 284 mL. Modern British recipes are metric.
- **The numbers come from a nutrition panel** → 240 mL. Labels use the US legal cup so serving sizes are consistent across brands.

One more signal that saves a lot of pain: if a recipe gives both cups and grams for the same ingredient, **trust the grams**. A cup measurement is a volume guess with a density assumption baked in; a gram figure is the author telling you exactly what they weighed.

## Cups to Milliliters by Ingredient

This is where the conversion stops being pure arithmetic. A cup is always the same volume in millilitres, but what that cup weighs depends entirely on what you put in it. The millilitre column never changes; the gram column does.

Honey stands out: a cup is 340 g, which is a full 44% heavier than the same cup of flour. If a recipe tells you "1 cup honey, 240 mL," it is simply saying 1 cup — the two notations are the same volume. It is when someone converts 1 cup of honey to 240 **grams** that the recipe breaks.

## How to Convert Cups to Milliliters Yourself

The arithmetic is short:

- **Cups → mL (US):** multiply by 236.6. *2½ cups × 236.6 = 591.5 mL.*
- **mL → cups (US):** divide by 236.6. *500 mL ÷ 236.6 = 2.11 cups.*
- **Cups → mL (metric):** multiply by 250.
- **Cups → mL (UK imperial):** multiply by 284.1.

Two rounding traps to avoid:

1. **Do not convert mid-recipe.** Convert one round-trip only. If you go cups → mL → cups you will drift, because 236.6 is not a round number.
2. **Round up only on non-critical liquids.** Stock, milk, and water tolerate a few millilitres of slop. Leavening, salt, and gelatine do not — keep those in grams.

## Milliliters Make Scaling Easier

Millilitres are what you want when you scale a recipe, for one reason: they are additive and linear. Cups are fraction-heavy (⅓, ⅔, ¾, ⅛) and the fractions do not divide cleanly. Multiply ⅔ cup by 1.5 and you get 1 cup — fine. Multiply ⅓ cup by 2.5 and you are stuck doing tenths of a cup in your head.

Compare:

| Original | ×1.5 | ×2 | In mL (×2, US) |
|----------|------|-----|----------------|
| ⅓ cup | ½ cup | ⅔ cup | 473 mL |
| ⅔ cup | 1 cup | 1⅓ cups | 631 mL |
| ¾ cup | 1 cup + 2 tbsp | 1½ cups | 355 mL |
| 1¼ cups | 1⅞ cups | 2½ cups | 592 mL |

This is also the reason European and professional formulas are written in grams and millilitres. ## Liquids vs Dry Ingredients: Do Not Use the Same Cup

A US liquid measuring cup is marked with a spout and headspace; a dry measuring cup is meant to be filled level and swept. Using one for the other costs you accuracy:

- **Liquid in a dry cup** → you cannot level it, and you will usually over-pour by 5–10%.
- **Dry in a liquid cup** → flour settles unevenly and you cannot sweep the top flat. Expect up to 25% too much flour.
