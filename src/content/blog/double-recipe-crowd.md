## Why Doubling a Recipe Isn't Just "Multiply by Two"

Hosting a big family dinner, a potluck, or a game-day party? Sooner or later every home cook faces the same problem: the recipe serves 6, and you have 14 hungry people arriving at seven o'clock. Knowing how to double a recipe for a crowd is one of the most useful kitchen skills you can learn — and it's also where a lot of dishes quietly go wrong.

For soups, stews, and casseroles, doubling usually works with simple multiplication. But baking, seasoning, and cook times don't always scale in a straight line. This guide walks you through the whole process, ingredient by ingredient, so your doubled batch tastes like the original — just bigger.

## Step 1: Start With the Base Multiplication

The foundation is easy: multiply every ingredient by 2. Where cooks stumble is converting awkward doubled measurements into something you can actually scoop. Here's a quick reference table:

| Original Amount | Doubled Amount |
|-----------------|----------------|
| ¼ cup | ½ cup |
| ⅓ cup | ⅔ cup |
| ½ cup | 1 cup |
| ¾ cup | 1½ cups |
| 1 tablespoon | 2 tablespoons (⅛ cup) |
| 1½ teaspoons | 1 tablespoon |
| ¾ teaspoon | 1½ teaspoons |

## Step 2: Know Which Ingredients Don't Double Cleanly

This is the part most "just multiply everything" advice skips. A handful of ingredients behave differently at larger volumes:

- **Salt and strong spices:** Start with 1.5x instead of 2x, then taste and adjust at the end. Salt perception doesn't scale linearly, and an over-salted pot of chili for 16 people is an expensive mistake.
- **Hot peppers and chili flakes:** Heat compounds in a bigger pot. Begin with 1.5x and add more after a simmer.
- **Baking soda and baking powder:** For most doubled baking recipes, 2x works — but if the original recipe already tastes slightly metallic or tangy, use 1.75x of the leavener.
- **Yeast:** Doubling the dough does not require doubling the yeast; 1.5x usually ferments on nearly the same schedule.
- **Garlic and fresh herbs:** These are forgiving. Double them fully — most crowds appreciate the extra flavor.
- **Alcohol (wine, brandy):** Use about 1.5x, since larger volumes evaporate proportionally less during cooking.

The pattern: bulk ingredients (flour, water, meat, vegetables) double exactly, while *potent* ingredients scale at roughly 1.5x with a taste-and-adjust finish.

## Step 3: Pick the Right Pan or Pot

Doubling volume without changing your cookware is the number-one cause of failed doubled bakes. A doubled cake batter in the original pan overflows or bakes raw in the center.

Two safe strategies:

1. **Use two identical pans.** Bake two 9-inch cakes instead of hunting for one giant pan. Timing stays identical to the original recipe — this is the easiest path.
2. **Move up in pan size correctly.** Pan capacity depends on area, not on the length of one side. An 8×8-inch pan (64 sq in) doubles almost exactly to a 9×13-inch pan (117 sq in). Our [Baking Pan Converter](/baking-pan-converter/) does this area math for you and suggests equivalent pans.

For stovetop cooking, make sure your pot is no more than two-thirds full after doubling. Overfilled pots simmer unevenly and take dramatically longer to reduce.

## How Do Cooking Times Change When You Double a Recipe?

This is the question everyone asks, and the answer depends on the cooking method:

- **Same pan size, two pans (baking):** Keep the original time and temperature. Rotate the pans halfway through if they share an oven.
- **One larger, deeper pan:** Keep the temperature, but expect 15–30% more time. Start checking at the original time and rely on doneness cues — a skewer test for cakes, internal temperature for meats.
- **Soups and braises:** The time to come to a simmer increases noticeably (more mass to heat), but the actual simmering time barely changes.
- **Roasting two items (e.g., two chickens):** Timing follows the size of *each* item, not the total weight. Two 4-pound chickens roast in roughly the time one 4-pound chicken needs, plus a few minutes for the crowded oven.

Never double the cook time just because you doubled the recipe — that's the fastest way to overcook dinner for 14.

## Step 4: Budget and Shop Smart for a Crowd

Doubling a recipe also doubles the grocery bill — and crowd cooking is where costs sneak up. Before you shop, price out the scaled ingredient list with our [Recipe Cost Calculator](/recipe-cost-calculator/) to see the per-serving cost. Two practical tips:

- Buy bulk ingredients (rice, pasta, chicken thighs) in family packs; the per-unit price often drops 20–30%.
- If a doubled recipe calls for an expensive ingredient in an awkward quantity (say, 1½ jars of something), round down and compensate with stock, herbs, or acid.

## A Worked Example: Doubling Chili for 12

Original recipe (serves 6): 2 lbs ground beef, 1 onion, 2 cloves garlic, 2 tbsp chili powder, 1 tsp salt, 2 cans (14 oz) tomatoes, 1 can beans.

Doubled for 12:

- 4 lbs ground beef, 2 onions, 4 cloves garlic — straight 2x
- 3 tbsp chili powder and 1½ tsp salt to start (1.5x), adjusted after 30 minutes of simmering
- 4 cans tomatoes, 2 cans beans — straight 2x
- Same simmer time, but about 15 extra minutes to reach a simmer in the bigger pot
- Brown the beef in two batches — crowding the pan steams the meat instead of searing it

That last point deserves emphasis: **double the ingredients, not the batch size in the pan.** Sear, sauté, and fry in the same batch sizes as the original recipe, just more times.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Doubling salt, chili, and leaveners blindly instead of scaling to 1.5x and tasting
- Using the original pan for doubled batter
- Doubling the cooking time "to be safe"
- Crowding the pan when browning meat
- Forgetting that a doubled recipe needs a bigger mixing bowl too (sounds obvious — until flour is on the floor)
