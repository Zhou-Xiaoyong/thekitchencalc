## How Many Grams in a Stick of Butter? The Short Answer

**One US stick of butter weighs 113 grams** — that is 4 ounces, 8 tablespoons, or ½ cup by volume. Everything else scales straight from that number: a half stick is 57 g, two sticks are 227 g, and the full one-pound box of four sticks is 454 g.

The number is tidy because it is a legal definition rather than a culinary accident. An American "stick" is a quarter pound. Since 1 lb = 453.592 g, a quarter of that is 113.4 g, and every US butter brand — Land O'Lakes, Challenge, Kerrygold's American line, and the store labels — wraps 113 g per stick and prints "4 oz" on the paper.

That single fact answers most questions. The rest of this page handles the cases where a recipe does not mean what you think it means.

## The Stick-to-Gram Chart

Keep this table within reach; it covers every fraction a recipe is likely to ask for.

| Sticks | Tablespoons | Cups | Grams | Ounces |
|--------|-------------|------|-------|--------|
| ¼ stick | 2 tbsp | ⅛ cup | 28 g | 1 oz |
| ½ stick | 4 tbsp | ¼ cup | 57 g | 2 oz |
| ⅔ stick | 5⅓ tbsp | ⅓ cup | 75 g | 2.7 oz |
| 1 stick | 8 tbsp | ½ cup | 113 g | 4 oz |
| 1½ sticks | 12 tbsp | ¾ cup | 170 g | 6 oz |
| 2 sticks | 16 tbsp | 1 cup | 227 g | 8 oz |
| 3 sticks | 24 tbsp | 1½ cups | 340 g | 12 oz |
| 4 sticks (1 lb) | 32 tbsp | 2 cups | 454 g | 16 oz |

Two rounding habits keep this chart honest. First, **113 g is a rounded 113.4 g** — over four sticks that is a 1.6 g shortfall, which no home recipe can detect. Second, **½ stick is 56.7 g, so round to 57 g**, not 56 g. Errors below 1% never matter in baking; errors above 3% occasionally do, and rounding the wrong direction twice in a row is how you get there.

## Why the Wrapper Sometimes Lies (or Rather, Why You Read It Wrong)

Not every "stick" is a quarter pound, and this is where conversions go sideways.

**Half-stick pats.** Some brands sell "half sticks" in a box of eight — each one is 2 oz / 57 g and is scored down the middle. If a recipe calls for "1 stick" and your refrigerator holds half sticks, you need two of them. Look for the number printed near the tear strip.

**European blocks.** Butter imported from Europe is sold as a *block*: 250 g (8.8 oz), 227 g (8 oz), or 200 g. A 250 g block measures slightly more than two US sticks, so a recipe asking for 227 g leaves you 23 g short of the whole block. The reverse case is just as common — a European recipe says "250 g butter" and you are holding two sticks totalling 227 g. That 23 g gap is 9%, enough to change a shortbread's texture.

**Canadian and Australian butter.** Sold in 454 g bricks with gram markings printed directly on the paper. There is no stick at all, so "1 stick" appearing in a Canadian reprint of a US recipe usually means 113 g and occasionally means ½ cup — which is the same thing.

**Whipped or spreadable butter.** These are aerated. A "stick" of whipped butter fills the same mould but weighs roughly 80 g, because a third of the volume is air. Never substitute it 1:1 for a solid stick by weight.

## How Many Grams in a Stick of Butter Without a Scale

You do not need a scale to get to 113 g, because US butter wrappers are already a measuring tool: each stick is printed with tablespoon marks, and **1 tablespoon of butter = 14.2 g**. Count eight marks and you have a stick.

Three other reliable methods:

- **Water displacement.** Fill a liquid measuring cup with 1 cup of cold water and drop the butter in. The rise in the water line tells you the volume: a rise of ½ cup means 113 g. This works for any solid fat and is how pastry chefs check a block quickly.
- **Use the tablespoon markings as a ruler.** Eight even segments down a stick — perfect for cutting 57 g without any maths.
- **Use the box arithmetic.** Most boxes carry four sticks and a net weight of 1 lb / 454 g. Cut the box in half and you have 227 g, whatever shape the butter is in.

## Why the Gram Count Matters More Than the Stick Count

Cooking tolerates a stick of slop. Baking frequently does not, and butter is one of the ingredients that punishes guessing hardest.

**Laminated dough.** Croissants, danishes, and puff pastry rely on a butter block that stays intact through dozens of folds. A butter block 10% too thick tears through the dough, and the layers leak. Recipes express the block in grams for exactly this reason — 227 g or 250 g, never "two sticks, roughly."

**Bread formulas.** Enriched doughs such as brioche and challah specify butter as a percentage of flour. If your flour is 500 g and the formula calls for 20% butter, that is 100 g — just under one stick. Rounding up to a full 113 g pushes you to 22.6%, which is a noticeably richer, heavier crumb. **Cookies and shortbread.** Butter is the structural fat. Swap 113 g for 125 g and the cookies spread more and bake thinner; swap down to 100 g and they stay domed and dry. There is no safety margin at the cheap end.
