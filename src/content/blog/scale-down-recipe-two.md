## Why Scaling Down Is Its Own Skill

Most recipes on the internet are written for four, six, or even eight people. That's great for Sunday dinner, but on a quiet night for two, you're left with the same problem: how do you turn a "serves 6" casserole into something that makes exactly two plates and not a week of leftovers?

Scaling down a recipe for two is the mirror image of doubling. The math is the same — multiply every ingredient by a ratio — but the pitfalls are different. Tiny amounts get fiddly, pans run half empty, and a third of a teaspoon is hard to measure. Here's how to do it cleanly.

When you halve a recipe, the easy ingredients cooperate: 2 cups becomes 1 cup, 1 pound becomes 8 ounces. But the moment the factor isn't a clean 0.5, things get weird:

- A "serves 4" recipe scaled to 2 is a clean **×0.5** factor.
- A "serves 3" recipe scaled to 2 is a **×0.67** factor — now 1 cup becomes about ⅔ cup.
- A "serves 6" recipe scaled to 2 is a **×0.33** factor — ¾ cup becomes ¼ cup.

Small amounts are where home cooks slip. A recipe calling for 1 teaspoon of baking powder, scaled to ⅓, wants "⅓ teaspoon" — which most measuring spoon sets don't include. You end up guessing, and in baking, guessing on leaveners changes the crumb.

Before you touch a single ingredient, decide your **scale factor**: `target servings ÷ original servings`. That one number drives everything.

## The Scale-Down Method, Step by Step

1. **Note the original yield.** Does the recipe say "serves 4" or "makes 24 cookies"? Use that number, not a guess.
2. **Calculate the factor.** For two people: `2 ÷ original`. Write it down so you don't forget mid-recipe.
3. **Multiply every ingredient.** Amounts, not just the big ones. Salt, spice, and yeast scale too — unless you're adjusting for a different pan (more on that below).
4. **Round sensibly.** Eggs and tiny spices don't divide perfectly. See the rounding table.
5. **Adjust the pan and the time.** Smaller batches cook faster and sit shallower. Don't skip this.

### Common Scale Factors for Two

| Original yield | Factor for 2 | Example: 2 cups flour becomes |
|----------------|--------------|------------------------------|
| Serves 4 | ×0.5 | 1 cup |
| Serves 6 | ×0.33 | ⅔ cup |
| Serves 3 | ×0.67 | 1⅓ cups |
| Makes 12 | ×0.17 | about 2 tbsp |
| Serves 8 | ×0.25 | ½ cup |

## The Egg Problem

Eggs are the classic scaling headache. You can't use "half an egg" easily, though you can: whisk one egg, weigh it (~50 g), and use 25 g. For most dishes, though, the practical move is to round to the nearest whole egg and nudge the liquid slightly.

| You need | Practical fix |
|----------|---------------|
| ½ egg | Whisk 1 egg, use half (≈25 g) |
| ⅓ egg | Whisk 1 egg, use ≈17 g |
| ¼ egg | Use 1 whole egg and reduce other liquids a touch |

## Don't Ignore the Pan

A half-size batch in a full-size pan spreads thin and over-bakes at the edges. This is the step people forget. After you've scaled the ingredients, check whether the new volume actually fits the pan you planned to use. Our [Baking Pan Converter](/baking-pan-converter/) compares pan areas so you can see whether to drop down a size or pull out a small ramekin.

As a rule of thumb: fill a baking dish somewhere between half and three-quarters full. Less than that and the edges scorch before the center sets.

## Units Matter More at Small Scale

When an amount is already small, rounding a tablespoon to the nearest teaspoon is a big relative error. Converting everything to grams first makes scaling exact, because grams divide cleanly — 15 g of butter at ×0.33 is 5 g, no guessing. ## Watch the Cook Time and the Cost

Smaller portions cook faster. A casserole that bakes 40 minutes at full size may be done in 25 at half size — start checking early and trust a thermometer over the clock. And because you're buying the same package sizes regardless, a two-person batch can actually cost *more* per serving than the full recipe. ## A Worked Example: Pasta Sauce for Two

Start with a sauce written for 4:
- 1 lb spaghetti, 2 tbsp olive oil, 4 garlic cloves, 28 oz tomatoes, ¼ cup basil, ½ cup parmesan, ½ tsp pepper.

Factor for 2 = 0.5:
- ½ lb spaghetti, 1 tbsp oil, 2 garlic cloves, 14 oz tomatoes, 2 tbsp basil, ¼ cup parmesan, ¼ tsp pepper.

That's a clean halving. A "serves 6" version of the same sauce would use the ×0.33 factor instead, giving roughly ⅓ lb pasta and 9 oz tomatoes — which is why the calculator beats mental math.
