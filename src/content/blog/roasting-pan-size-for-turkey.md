## A 20-Pound Bird Fills 17 Inches of Pan

A 20-pound Thanksgiving turkey measures about 16 inches from the tail to the top of the breastbone, and it wants 17 by 13 inches of pan interior with sides 3 inches deep. Anything smaller and the legs hang over the rim.

The pan is the one thing people buy in the last week, usually in a store aisle with no way to test the fit. Here is the fit, worked out on paper.

## Measure the Bird, Not the Label

A raw whole turkey is longer than it looks in the package because the legs are tucked. Approximate raw dimensions, breast up and legs trussed:

| Bird | Length | Width at the hips | Smallest interior | Typical pan label | Pan volume |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 to 12 lb | 13 in | 8 in | 15 by 11 in | 14 by 10, "small" | 6 qt |
| 12 to 16 lb | 14 to 15 in | 9 in | 16 by 12 in | 16 by 13 | 8 qt |
| 16 to 20 lb | 16 in | 9 to 10 in | 17 by 13 in | 17 by 13 | 10 qt |
| 20 to 24 lb | 17 in | 10 in | 18 by 14 in | 18 by 14, "large" | 12 qt |

Two inches of clearance on every side is the working number. Less than that and the hot oven air can't move between the bird and the pan wall, so the side facing the wall stays pale while the breast browns.

Pan labels describe the outside. A pan sold as 17 by 13 usually has about 16 by 12 of usable floor, and the handles stick out another inch at each end. That is why a 20-pounder rated for a 17-inch pan often needs the 18-inch one.

## What a 9x13 Actually Holds

A 9-by-13 glass or metal baker has 117 square inches of floor and sides about 2 inches high. That is a 12-pound bird at the absolute limit, breast up, legs tucked in tight, and it is the wrong pan for anything larger.

Three things go wrong past 12 pounds:

- The legs drape over the rim and scorch against the oven rack above.
- The bird's top sits 4 to 5 inches above the pan floor, close enough to the heating element to brown before the thigh reaches 175°F.
- Two cups of drippings spread across 117 square inches sit an eighth of an inch deep and burn onto the glass within 40 minutes.

A 14-pound bird in a 9x13 will cook. It won't brown evenly, and the pan will take a two-day soak.

## The Half Sheet Trap

An 18-by-13 half sheet holds a 20-pound bird, which makes it tempting. The rim is the problem. A half sheet has a 1-inch lip, so the same two cups of drippings spread out about a tenth of an inch deep, scorch, and smoke out the kitchen by the 90-minute mark.

A half sheet does work for one thing: a butterflied bird. Spatchcock a 14-pounder, press it flat, and it fits 18 by 13 with room to spare and browns in about 80 minutes at 425°F because the whole surface is exposed. That is a different recipe, not a pan substitution.

If you use a sheet pan for a whole bird anyway, set a rack in it and keep a cup of water in the bottom to hold the drippings back from the metal.

## Depth, Handles, and the Lifting Problem

Three-inch sides hold 2 cups of drippings without a wave going over the edge when the pan tilts. A 20-pound bird plus a heavy pan weighs close to 25 pounds, and it comes out of a 325°F oven on a rack you reach into with two hands.

Disposable aluminum pans are the failure point here. The common 16-by-11 size is rated for 12 to 14 pounds, and a 20-pound bird buckles the floor of the pan as you lift it. Nest two pans, or slide a sheet pan underneath before the bird goes in, never after.

## The Oven Clearance Check Nobody Does

A standard 30-inch range has roughly 24 inches of usable rack width and 18 inches of depth, and the back wall usually has a broiler bump or a fan housing that steals 2 more.

Measure the rack, then subtract 2 inches for the door's inner panel. A 20-inch pan with handles leaves about 2 inches of air on each side, which is enough. A 22-inch pan in the same oven sits against both walls, and the bird's back side stops browning.

The pan also owns the best rack. A 17-by-13 roasting pan takes the middle of the only shelf wide enough to hold it, which leaves no room for the 9-by-13 of stuffing beside it. ## Disposable, Enamel, or Tri-Ply

The price spread is enormous, and the decision comes down to how many birds you roast.

| Option | Price | Weight it holds | Lifespan | What it does well |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Disposable aluminum, 16 by 11 | $3 to $6 | 12 to 14 lb | One bird | Nothing to wash, nothing to store |
| Enamel on steel, 16 by 13 | $25 to $45 | 18 to 20 lb | 10 years or more | Light enough to lift, browns well, cheap to replace |
| Cast aluminum with a rack | $50 to $80 | 25 lb | Decades | Even heat, no hot spots on the pan floor |
| Tri-ply stainless, 18 by 14 | $90 to $200 | 25 lb | Decades | Deglazes on the stovetop, oven to burner in one pan |

Two birds a year for ten years is 20 disposable pans, roughly $80, plus a sheet pan under each one to keep it from folding. Enamel costs less than that and lifts without drama.

Tri-ply earns its price on one feature: you can set it over two burners and deglaze the fond for gravy without transferring anything. If gravy is the part of the meal you care about, that is the argument.

## A Buying Checklist for the Week Before

Standing in the aisle, in order:

- **Bird weight first.** 12 pounds or less, a 15-by-11 pan. 16 pounds, a 16-by-13. 20 pounds, 17-by-13 or larger.
- **Interior dimensions, not the label.** Bring a tape measure. The label is the outside.
- **Three-inch sides.** Under 2 inches and the drippings scorch.
- **A rack in the box.** A V-rack holds the bird steady and lets the bottom brown. No rack means the bird poaches in its own fat.
- **Handles clear your oven.** Measure the rack before you go, not after.
- **Lift it loaded.** A pan you can't hold steady with 20 pounds in it is a burn waiting to happen.

## FAQ

### What size roasting pan do I need for a 20-pound turkey?

A 17-by-13-inch pan with 3-inch sides, or an 18-by-14 if the pan has wide handles. You need about 2 inches of clearance around a bird that measures 16 inches long and 10 inches across the hips, and enough depth to hold 2 cups of drippings.

### Can I roast a turkey in a 9x13 pan?

Only up to about 12 pounds. A 9x13 has 117 square inches of floor and 2-inch sides, so a larger bird's legs hang over the rim and the drippings scorch. For a 14-pound bird, use a 16-by-13 roasting pan or butterfly the bird and roast it on a half sheet.

### Do I need a rack in the roasting pan?

Yes, if you want the bottom of the bird to brown rather than sit in fat. A V-rack holds a whole turkey steady and lets air and heat under it. Without one, put the bird on a bed of onion, carrot, and celery, which lifts it an inch and flavors the drippings.
