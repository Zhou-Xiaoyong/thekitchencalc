## What Is 1 Tablespoon in Grams?

It depends on what you are measuring. **1 tablespoon of all-purpose flour is 7.5 grams; 1 tablespoon of granulated sugar is 12.5 grams; 1 tablespoon of butter is 14.2 grams; 1 tablespoon of water is 15 grams.** A tablespoon is a fixed volume (14.79 mL in the US), so the only thing that changes between ingredients is **density** — how much that 14.79 mL actually weighs.

This is the trap most online conversion charts fall into. They give you one number for "1 tablespoon → grams" and either ignore the ingredient or hedge with a wide range (10–17 g) that is useless in a baking context. Real recipes need the specific number for the specific ingredient.

## Why Density Matters More Than the Number on the Chart

The honest answer to "how many grams is 1 tablespoon" is "of what?" If you weigh 1 tablespoon of flour with a fine scale you get 7.5 g if you spoon and level it, but 8–10 g if you scoop — the same error that messes up cup measurements. **Tablespoons magnify that mistake because the volume is small: a packed tablespoon is 30–40% heavier than a level one.** So a 25% error at the tablespoon level becomes a 25% error in your recipe, not the dilution you get with cups.

The reverse mistake is also common: assuming a tablespoon of honey and a tablespoon of water weigh the same. They do not — honey is 21.25 g vs water at 15 g, because honey is denser (about 1.42 g/mL). In a recipe that uses a lot of honey (glazes, baklava, teriyaki), assuming "1 tbsp is 1 tbsp" can leave you 30% short on sugar and overrun on liquid.

## Three Quick Rules That Survive Any Ingredient

Even when you do not have a chart in front of you, three density tiers cover most pantry ingredients:

- **Light powders (≈ 7 g/tbsp):** all-purpose and cake flour, cocoa, powdered sugar, oats. Anything that "pours and settles."
- **Mid-density (≈ 12–15 g/tbsp):** granulated sugar, brown sugar, butter, salt, baking powder, oil, water, milk, shredded cheese.
- **Heavy syrups (≈ 20–22 g/tbsp):** honey, maple syrup, molasses, corn syrup. About 2× the weight of flour at the same volume.

If you remember nothing else, remembering these three tiers will get you within 5% of any ingredient you can name.

## When to Convert — and When to Skip

If you are scaling a recipe, you should convert tablespoons to grams **once at the top of the recipe and scale in grams from there**, not for every ingredient. Round-tripping (cups → grams → cups as you go) multiplies the rounding error each time. Convert flour, sugar, butter, and salt; leave vanilla, extracts, and "a pinch of" as-is — those are flavor, not structure.

If the recipe yields 36 cookies but you need 60, multiply each weight by 1.67 and divide the dough into 60 portions instead of eyeballing "add another half batch."
