## How to Measure Grams Without a Scale: The Core Idea

A recipe gives you a number in grams, your drawer has no scale, and the dough is waiting. Fortunately, **grams are just mass**, and mass can be reproduced by volume as long as you know the density of what you are measuring. Every method below is that one equation in a different costume:

> **grams = volume (mL) × density (g/mL)**

Water, milk, stock, oil, and vinegar are easy because their density sits near 1.0. Flour, sugar, and cocoa are the hard cases because a cup of each weighs something different, and the way you scoop can swing the result by 20%.

Below are seven practical ways to hit a gram target with the tools already in your kitchen, ordered from most to least accurate.

## Method 1: Use a Measuring Jug for Anything Water-Based

For liquids that are mostly water — milk, stock, wine, juice, vinegar — the density is 1.00–1.03 g/mL. That is close enough that **1 mL equals 1 gram** for any home recipe.

- 240 g of milk → 240 mL on the jug (about 1 cup)
- 500 g of stock → 500 mL
- 100 g of white wine → 100 mL

This is the single most accurate no-scale method, because a jug measures volume to within about 1–2%. Just read it at eye level, at the bottom of the meniscus, on a flat counter.

## Method 2: Build a Balance Scale From Two Coins

If you need true weight rather than volume, a homemade balance gets you there. You need a straight rod, a pivot (a pencil on a glass rim works), two identical cups, and **US coins**, whose weights are fixed by law:

| Coin | Weight |
|------|--------|
| Penny (post-1982) | 2.5 g |
| Nickel | 5.0 g |
| Dime (post-1965) | 2.268 g |
| Quarter (post-1965) | 5.67 g |

Four quarters and a penny is 25.18 g; twenty nickels is exactly 100 g. Put the coins in one cup, your ingredient in the other, and add ingredient until the arms level. It is slow, it is fiddly, and for a 50 g target it is genuinely accurate to within a gram or two. Save it for yeast, salt, and leavening, where small errors matter most.

## Method 3: Work Backwards From the Package Label

Every sealed package in the US prints a net weight in both ounces and grams. That gives you a free scale:

- A 454 g (1 lb) bag of flour divided into 4 equal portions is 113 g each.
- A 340 g bag of chocolate chips split into 2 is 170 g per half.
- A 397 g can of condensed milk is 397 g, full stop — no measuring needed.
- A 7 g packet of yeast is 7 g.

This is the best method for anything sold in a single-use pack. Divide by eye, then divide again: halving a pile repeatedly is much more accurate than trying to eyeball a third.

## Method 4: Convert With a Density Chart and Your Measuring Cups

When the recipe wants 125 g of flour, you need the grams-per-cup figure for that specific ingredient. These are the values used in most US baking references:

| Ingredient | 1 cup (grams) | 1 tbsp (grams) | 1 tsp (grams) |
|------------|---------------|----------------|---------------|
| All-purpose flour | 125 | 8 | 2.6 |
| Bread flour | 127 | 8 | 2.7 |
| Whole wheat flour | 120 | 7.5 | 2.5 |
| Granulated sugar | 200 | 12.5 | 4.2 |
| Brown sugar (packed) | 220 | 13.8 | 4.6 |
| Powdered sugar | 120 | 7.5 | 2.5 |
| Butter | 227 | 14.2 | 4.7 |
| Rolled oats | 90 | 5.6 | 1.9 |
| Cocoa powder | 85 | 5.3 | 1.8 |
| Honey | 340 | 21 | 7 |
| Rice (uncooked) | 185 | 11.6 | 3.9 |
| Shredded cheese | 113 | 7 | 2.3 |

Read the chart as division: 250 g of flour ÷ 125 g per cup = 2 cups. 15 g of butter ÷ 14.2 g per tbsp ≈ 1 tbsp. The [Kitchen Measurement Converter](/kitchen-measurement-converter/) does this arithmetic for 16 ingredients at once, including the awkward ones like cocoa and chopped nuts.

Scoop correctly and this method lands within about 5%. Scoop badly and it lands within 25% — see the last section.

## Method 5: Use Spoons as a Small Precision Scale

For anything under 20 g, tablespoons are a better instrument than cups, because the smaller the volume, the smaller the absolute error. Memorise four numbers and you can weigh salt, yeast, baking powder, and spices by spoon:

- **Salt:** 1 tsp fine salt = 6 g; 1 tsp Diamond Crystal kosher = 2.8 g; 1 tsp Morton kosher = 4.8 g
- **Instant yeast:** 1 tsp = 3.1 g; 1 tbsp = 9.3 g
- **Active dry yeast:** 1 tsp = 3.1 g (a 7 g packet is 2¼ tsp)
- **Baking powder:** 1 tsp = 4 g
- **Baking soda:** 1 tsp = 4.6 g

Note the kosher salt split. Diamond Crystal and Morton differ by 70% by volume, which is why a "1 tsp salt" instruction is meaningless without the brand — and why the [Sugar & Salt Calculator](/sugar-salt-calculator/) asks you to pick one.

## Method 6: Divide a Known Batch Evenly

If you are making a whole recipe rather than measuring an ingredient, you can skip grams entirely and divide the finished mass. Weigh nothing, but know the total: a 1 kg bag of dough split into 8 pieces is 125 g per piece, and a 500 g block of butter cut into 20 slices is 25 g each. Use a ruler for the cuts and you will be accurate to a few percent, which is plenty for portioning bread, cookie dough, and burger patties.

## Method 7: Estimate by Hand (Last Resort)

Handfuls and palms are for seasoning, not baking. Still, the rough figures are worth knowing: a cupped handful of flour is about 40 g, a palm of salt about 5 g, a thumb-tip of butter about 10 g. Use these to finish a stew. Never use them for a cake.

## Where the Error Actually Comes From

Volume-based measuring fails for one reason: **settling**. Flour compacts in the bag during shipping, so a scoop straight from the bag can weigh 160 g where the chart says 125 g. Fix it in three steps:

1. **Fluff** the flour with a fork before measuring.
2. **Spoon** it into the cup rather than digging the cup in.
3. **Level** with the flat edge of a knife — never tap or shake the cup down.

Do those three things and a cup of flour lands at 120–130 g. Skip them and your "125 g" cup silently becomes 160 g, a 28% error that turns a tender cake into a brick. If you bake often enough to care, a $15 digital scale pays for itself in the first month; until then, spoon-and-level plus a chart is the honest next best thing.
