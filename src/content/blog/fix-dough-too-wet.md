## Your Hand Comes Out Coated

A 500 g batch of bread flour and 350 g of water, mixed six minutes, should pull off your palm with a light film. When it coats you to the wrist and the dough has spread to the rim of the bowl, the instinct is to reach for the flour bag.

Wait ten minutes and retest. Roughly half the doughs that feel too wet at minute six are underdeveloped rather than over-watered, and another quarter are just cold. Flour fixes neither one.

**Dry-to-touch is not a percentage.** What your hand reads is how much water the starch has taken up and how much gluten has formed around it. Both move a lot in the first hour and barely at all after that.

## Three Tests That Take Five Minutes

Run these before you change the formula.

| Test | How | Reads wet | Reads underdeveloped |
|---|---|---|---|
| Windowpane | Stretch a walnut-sized piece between your thumbs | Tear is clean, thin patches go translucent | Tears ragged at 2 inches, no translucent patch |
| Bowl clear | Stand mixer, 6 min at medium speed | Still climbing the bowl at 8 min | Clears the bowl, then sags when you stop |
| Spread | 20 min after mixing, 75°F room | Spreads more than an inch, wet sheen on top | Holds a mound, surface goes from shiny to satin |

The windowpane decides it. A dough that stretches to 4 inches without tearing has a network, and a network holds water. If it rips at 2 inches into a ragged edge, water is not your problem and more flour will only give you a stiff dough with no structure.

## Why Undeveloped Dough Reads as Wet

Gluten is what turns free water into something you can shape. Before it forms, water sits free between starch granules.

Ten minutes of folds does more for the bench feel than 20 g of flour. Try it on one batch: mix to shaggy, rest 20 minutes, then run four sets of stretch-and-folds 30 minutes apart. Each set tightens the dough visibly. By the third, most 72% doughs stop sticking to a dry hand.

Machine time is the shortcut: two minutes on the lowest speed, then six at medium. Stopping at four leaves a dough that looks mixed and handles like soup.

## Thirty Minutes in the Fridge Changes No Numbers

This is the cheapest fix here, and it does not alter a single percentage.

A dough mixed at 76°F and left in the bowl feels slack. Put the covered bowl in a 38°F fridge for 30 minutes and the same dough drops into the low 60s. Cold gluten is stiffer. A 78% dough straight out of that chill shapes like a 72% one for about eight minutes at the bench before it warms back up.

Use the window instead of fighting it. Flour the bench lightly, shape fast, and get the loaf into its pan while it is still cold. If you are already at the shaping stage and the dough is spreading, this is the only fix that works without touching the formula.

## When You Genuinely Need More Flour

Sometimes the water is above what your flour will take. Add flour in 15 g steps, weigh it, and know what each one costs.

Starting point: 500 g bread flour, 375 g water, 10 g salt, 6 g yeast. That is 75.0%.

| Flour added | New flour weight | New hydration | Salt drops to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 g | 515 g | 72.8% | 1.94% |
| 30 g | 530 g | 70.8% | 1.89% |
| 45 g | 545 g | 68.8% | 1.83% |
| 64 g (½ cup) | 564 g | 66.5% | 1.77% |

That last row is what happens when you grab the measuring cup. Half a cup of bread flour is 64 g on our [conversion chart](/conversion-chart/), so one splash takes a 75% dough to 66.5% and cuts the salt by more than a tenth. Yeast drops the same way, which is why a rescued dough often proofs slower.

There is a second cost. Bench flour lands outside the dough. Knead it 30 seconds and you build a skin that feels right over a core that is still slack. Fold instead, then wait 15 minutes before judging.

## Autolyse Does the Same Job With No Flour

If you have 30 minutes to spare, spend them before the mix rather than after. Stir the flour and the full water weight together, cover, and leave it. No salt, no yeast.

The first 10 minutes look worse, wetter and lumpier than the bowl did a moment ago. By 30 minutes the flour has taken up most of the water it will ever take and the mass stiffens, so a 70% dough handles like something nearer 67%. Salt and yeast go in afterward, and the mix time drops by two or three minutes.

Past about two hours the benefit stops and the dough starts to slacken again, faster with whole grain. Set a timer instead of leaving it for the afternoon.

## Weigh the Finished Dough Once

Your 500/350/10/6 batch should weigh 866 g. If the scale reads 890 g, then 24 g of water got in somewhere and the formula was never the problem. It comes from water clinging to the bowl when you tare with the bowl already loaded, or from a scale that needs a fresh zero.

## Over-Proofed Dough Also Looks Wet

A dough that spreads at the end of bulk may not hold too much water. It may have run out of structure.

Poke it. Flour a finger, press half an inch in, hold three seconds, pull out. A dent that fills back halfway is ready. A dent that stays, with the dough sighing down around it, means the gluten has degraded and bench flour will not rebuild it.

Shape that dough straight into a pan, where the walls carry the structure it lost, and bake at 375°F. Or spoon 200 g into tomorrow's batch as a pre-ferment. At 70% hydration that piece carries 116 g of flour and 81 g of water, so subtract both from the new formula and let the [sourdough calculator](/sourdough-calculator/) keep the totals straight.

## When It Is a Different Bread

Some doughs cannot be fixed because they were never meant to be loaves.

Above 80%, stop shaping and start folding in the bowl. Four sets at 30-minute intervals, then tip the mass onto a heavily floured couche, cut it into slippers, and bake at 475°F with steam for 20 to 24 minutes. That is ciabatta, and it wants the water you are trying to take out.

The harder diagnosis sits at the other end. A dough that will not clear the bowl at 65%, or one that still tears at 2 inches after 20 minutes of folding, usually has a flour problem: a low-protein all-purpose, a bag open since spring, or a bleached flour that never developed well. All-purpose at 10 to 11% protein absorbs around 60%. Bread flour at 12.5 to 13% takes 65 to 68. Whole wheat needs another 8 to 10 points because the bran drinks. Rye goes further.

Change the flour, or accept the lower percentage. Once you land on a number you can repeat, the [dough hydration calculator](/dough-hydration-calculator/) will hold it for the next batch.

## The Bake Changes Too

A wet dough that survives shaping still needs a different bake. Steam does the early work: 20 minutes with the lid on at 475°F, then 22 to 25 minutes uncovered for a 900 g loaf. A 70% loaf of the same weight needs roughly 20 and 18. Pull at 205 to 210°F, because the extra water has to leave before the crumb sets.

Then wait two hours on a wire rack. A high-hydration loaf cut at 30 minutes steams from the inside, and the slice you were proud of turns to paste on the board.
