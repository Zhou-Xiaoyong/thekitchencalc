## Convert Grams to Cups Without a Scale: The Core Idea

You do not need a scale to convert grams to cups. You need one number per ingredient: **grams per cup**. Divide, and you are done.

```
cups = grams ÷ (grams per cup)
```

So 300 g of all-purpose flour is 300 ÷ 120 = **2.5 cups**. That is the entire method. Everything below is about picking the right divisor and measuring the cup accurately enough that the divisor means something.

Here are the numbers you actually use most, based on the standard US cup (236.6 mL) and the spoon-and-level technique:

| Ingredient | Grams per cup | Grams per tablespoon |
|------------|---------------|----------------------|
| All-purpose flour | 120 g | 7.5 g |
| Bread flour | 127 g | 8 g |
| Whole wheat flour | 120 g | 7.5 g |
| Granulated sugar | 200 g | 12.5 g |
| Brown sugar (packed) | 220 g | 13.75 g |
| Powdered sugar | 120 g | 7.5 g |
| Butter | 227 g | 14.2 g |
| Water | 237 g | 14.8 g |
| Milk | 244 g | 15.3 g |
| Vegetable oil | 216 g | 13.5 g |
| Honey | 340 g | 21 g |
| Cocoa powder | 85 g | 5.3 g |
| Rolled oats | 90 g | 5.6 g |
| Chocolate chips | 170 g | 10.6 g |
| Rice (uncooked) | 185 g | 11.6 g |
| Peanut butter | 258 g | 16 g |

Two important caveats before you start dividing. First, **flour is the wild card** — depending on whether you scoop, spoon, or sift, a cup of flour runs anywhere from 100 g to 150 g. The 120 g figure assumes you spoon it in and level it. Second, **a cup of liquid and a cup of dry goods are measured in different cups**. More on both below.

## Method 1: The Grams-per-Cup Division

This is the default and it is accurate to within about 5% for everything except flour, where your technique dominates the error.

Worked examples:

- **500 g flour → cups:** 500 ÷ 120 = **4.17 cups** (4 cups + 2 tbsp)
- **250 g sugar → cups:** 250 ÷ 200 = **1.25 cups** (1¼ cups)
- **150 g butter → cups:** 150 ÷ 227 = **0.66 cups** (⅔ cup, or 10½ tbsp)
- **80 g cocoa → cups:** 80 ÷ 85 = **0.94 cups** (just under 1 cup)
- **400 g honey → cups:** 400 ÷ 340 = **1.18 cups** (1 cup + 3 tbsp)

## Method 2: Use Tablespoons as a Tiny Scale

A tablespoon is a reliable spoonful once you standardize it: level, not heaped. Since there are 16 tablespoons in a cup, you can convert grams to tablespoons and count.

- 120 g flour ÷ 7.5 g per tbsp = **16 tbsp = 1 cup**
- 200 g sugar ÷ 12.5 g per tbsp = **16 tbsp = 1 cup**
- 227 g butter ÷ 14.2 g per tbsp = **16 tbsp = 1 cup**

The pattern is useful: for most dry ingredients, the grams-per-tablespoon number is exactly the grams-per-cup number divided by 16, so a cup is always 16 level tablespoons. This method shines for small amounts — 30 g of cocoa powder is 5½ tablespoons, which is much easier to measure than 0.35 of a cup.

For butter specifically, skip the spoons. Every US butter stick is printed with tablespoon markings, and each segment is 14.2 g. Cut on the lines and you have a gram-accurate portion with no scale at all.

## Method 3: Water Displacement (for Water-Like Ingredients)

Because 1 mL of water weighs 1 g, a liquid measuring cup becomes a scale for anything with roughly the density of water. Set the cup on the counter, pour until the water line reads the milliliter number that matches your gram target, and you have weighed it.

This works cleanly for water, milk, stock, broth, juice, wine, and vinegar — all within 5% of water's density. It does not work for oil (about 0.92 g/mL, so 100 g of oil fills 109 mL) or honey (1.42 g/mL, so 100 g fills only 70 mL). For those, use the division method instead.

The rule of thumb: **grams ÷ density = milliliters.** Water and milk: divide by 1. Oil: divide by 0.92. Honey and syrup: divide by 1.42.

## Method 4: Convert Grams to Fluid Ounces First

If your measuring cup is marked in ounces rather than milliliters, route through fluid ounces. One fluid ounce is 29.57 mL, so one cup is 8 fl oz.

- 237 g water ≈ 8 fl oz = 1 cup
- 244 g milk ≈ 8.25 fl oz = 1 cup
- 216 g oil ≈ 7.3 fl oz = 1 cup

For dry ingredients you are now using fluid ounces as a proxy for volume, which is fine — the conversion is about volume, not mass. Just be consistent about the cup you use to measure.

## Getting the Cup Measurement Right (This Is Where Errors Come From)

The formula is exact; your cup is not. Three habits close most of the gap:

- **Spoon and level dry ingredients.** Spoon the flour into a dry measuring cup, then sweep the top flat with the back of a knife. Never scoop directly from the bag and never tap the cup down.
- **Use a dry cup for dry, a liquid cup for liquid.** A liquid measuring cup is wider and has headroom; using it for flour packs the flour into the corners and overshoots by up to 20%.
- **Pack brown sugar on purpose.** Brown sugar is the one ingredient you should compress — press it until it holds the shape of the cup when you turn it out. 1 cup packed brown sugar is 220 g; loose brown sugar is closer to 145 g, which is a big swing in a cookie recipe.

If your recipe is weight-based and you have no scale, convert each ingredient separately. Do not convert the total and divide — that only works if every ingredient shares a density, and they never do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I just use the 1 cup = 200 g rule for everything?**
No. That is roughly right for granulated sugar and wrong by more than 60% for cocoa powder. Ingredient density varies by a factor of four across a normal pantry.

**Does sifting change the conversion?**
Yes, upward. Sifted flour is fluffier and lighter, so 1 cup of sifted all-purpose flour is about 110 g, and 1 cup of sifted cake flour is about 100 g. If a recipe specifies sifted, use the lower divisor.

**Why does my recipe still taste off?**
Usually leavening or salt, not flour. Small quantities compound faster than the base ingredient does — a 5% flour error is invisible, but a 5% baking powder error is not. **What about metric cups?**
An Australian or Canadian metric cup is 250 mL, so 1 cup of water is 250 g and 1 cup of flour is about 127 g. Use the metric divisors if the recipe comes from a site that uses metric cups.
