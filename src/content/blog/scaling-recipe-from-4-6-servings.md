## Why 4 to 6 Is the Trickiest Scale-Up

Doubling a recipe is easy — everything is × 2 and the fractions stay clean. Halving is almost as simple. But scaling a recipe from 4 to 6 servings means multiplying by 1.5, and that's exactly the factor that produces measurements no one wants to deal with: 1.5 eggs, ⅜ teaspoon, 1⅛ cups.

Two extra guests is the most common scaling problem in home kitchens, and it deserves a proper method rather than eyeballing. This guide gives you the math, the awkward-number workarounds, and the short list of ingredients that should *not* get the full 1.5x treatment.

## Step 1: The Base Math — Multiply Everything by 1.5

The scaling factor is simply new servings ÷ old servings: 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5**. Here's a reference table for the measurements you'll hit most often:

| Original (serves 4) | × 1.5 (serves 6) | Practical Measure |
|---------------------|------------------|-------------------|
| ¼ cup | ⅜ cup | ¼ cup + 2 tbsp |
| ⅓ cup | ½ cup | ½ cup |
| ½ cup | ¾ cup | ¾ cup |
| ⅔ cup | 1 cup | 1 cup |
| ¾ cup | 1⅛ cups | 1 cup + 2 tbsp |
| 1 cup | 1½ cups | 1½ cups |
| 1½ cups | 2¼ cups | 2¼ cups |
| 2 cups | 3 cups | 3 cups |
| 1 tsp | 1½ tsp | ½ tbsp |
| 1½ tsp | 2¼ tsp | ¾ tbsp |
| 1 tbsp | 1½ tbsp | 1 tbsp + 1½ tsp |
| ¼ tsp | ⅜ tsp | ¼ tsp + ⅛ tsp |

Two conversions worth memorizing, because they solve most 1.5x headaches: **3 teaspoons = 1 tablespoon** and **16 tablespoons = 1 cup**. Any awkward fraction of a cup can be expressed in tablespoons, and any awkward fraction of a tablespoon in teaspoons.

## How Do You Handle 1.5 Eggs?

This is the single most-asked question about scaling from 4 to 6, and there are three legitimate answers.

**Option 1 — Weigh it (most accurate).** A large egg is about 50 g without the shell: roughly 30 g white, 20 g yolk. Crack two eggs into a bowl, beat them thoroughly, then weigh out 75 g. That's your 1.5 eggs, precisely.

**Option 2 — Split by component.** Use 1 whole egg + 1 yolk for richer results (custards, brownies, enriched dough), or 1 whole egg + 1 white for lighter, more structural results (sponges, meringue-adjacent bakes). This is the pastry chef's shortcut.

**Option 3 — Round up and compensate.** Use 2 whole eggs and reduce another liquid by about 2 tablespoons. Perfectly acceptable for pancakes, muffins, and most casseroles where a little extra moisture won't ruin anything.

For anything where structure matters — cakes, choux, custards — use Option 1 or 2. For everyday cooking, Option 3 is fine.

## Step 2: Know What Doesn't Scale at 1.5x

Bulk ingredients scale linearly. Potent ones don't. Here's the practical breakdown:

- **Flour, sugar, milk, stock, meat, vegetables, pasta, rice:** full 1.5x. No adjustment needed.
- **Salt:** start at 1.25x, taste at the end, adjust up. Salt perception is not linear, and a 1.5x pot of soup can taste over-seasoned.
- **Chili, cayenne, black pepper:** 1.25x to start. Heat concentrates as volume grows.
- **Baking powder and baking soda:** 1.4x is safer than 1.5x. Over-leavened batter rises fast and collapses.
- **Yeast:** 1.25x is plenty. The dough will ferment on nearly the same schedule.
- **Vanilla, almond extract, and other concentrated flavorings:** 1.25x. These get overwhelming quickly.
- **Garlic, fresh herbs, citrus zest:** full 1.5x or more. Forgiving and usually welcome.
- **Wine, brandy, vinegar:** 1.25x, since larger volumes reduce proportionally less.

The mental model: if the ingredient is measured in cups, multiply by 1.5. If it's measured in teaspoons, start at 1.25x and correct by taste.

## Step 3: Weigh Instead of Measuring

Scaling by 1.5 is the moment volume measurement starts failing you. Cup fractions compound errors — a slightly heaped ¾ cup, multiplied and re-measured, drifts far enough to change texture.

Convert to grams first, scale, then bake. A cup of all-purpose flour is 120 g, so 1.5 cups is 180 g — a number you can hit exactly on a scale. ## Step 4: Check Your Pan and Pot

A 1.5x batch needs about 50% more space, and this is where scale-ups quietly fail.

- **Stovetop:** your pot should be no more than two-thirds full after scaling. If it isn't, move up a size — an overfilled pot simmers unevenly and takes much longer to reduce.
- **Baking:** a 1.5x cake batter will overflow the original pan. An 8×8-inch pan (64 sq in) scaled by 1.5 needs about 96 sq in — a 9×9 (81 sq in) isn't enough, but a 9×13 (117 sq in) works with a slightly thinner bake. - **Roasting and searing:** do *not* scale the batch size in the pan. Brown meat and sauté aromatics in the same-size batches as the original recipe — just do it more times. Crowding steams instead of searing.

## Does Cooking Time Change From 4 to 6 Servings?

Usually less than people expect:

- **Soups, stews, sauces:** the simmer time stays the same. Only the time to *reach* a simmer increases, by roughly 5–10 minutes.
- **Same-depth bakes in a wider pan:** keep the original time, start checking 5 minutes early.
- **Deeper bakes in the same footprint:** add 15–25% more time and drop the temperature by 25°F (15°C).
- **Roasts:** timing follows the weight of each individual piece, not the total. A 1.5x quantity of chicken thighs on a bigger sheet pan cooks in nearly the original time.

Never multiply cooking time by 1.5. That's the fastest way to overcook a scaled-up dinner.

## A Worked Example: Pasta Bake, 4 to 6

Original (serves 4): 250 g pasta, 400 g tomatoes, 1 onion, 2 cloves garlic, 1 tsp salt, ½ tsp chili flakes, 150 g cheese, 2 tbsp olive oil.

Scaled to 6:

- 375 g pasta, 600 g tomatoes, 225 g cheese, 3 tbsp olive oil — straight 1.5x
- 1½ onions (use 2 small ones), 3 cloves garlic — straight 1.5x
- 1¼ tsp salt and ⅝ tsp chili flakes to start, adjusted before baking — 1.25x
- Move from an 8×8 to a 9×13 dish
- Bake the same time and temperature; the layer is slightly thinner, so check 5 minutes early
