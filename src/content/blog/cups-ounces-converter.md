## Cups to Ounces: The Short Answer

**1 cup = 8 fluid ounces = 236.6 mL.** That part is fixed and never changes, because it is a volume-to-volume conversion.

The trouble starts the moment a recipe says "ounces" without telling you which kind. There are two:

- **Fluid ounces (fl oz)** measure **volume**. One cup of anything — water, flour, honey, gravel — is 8 fl oz.
- **Weight ounces (oz)** measure **mass**. One cup of water is 8.3 oz, one cup of all-purpose flour is 4.2 oz, one cup of honey is 12 oz.

So "cups to ounces" has two completely different answers depending on which ounce you mean. This guide covers both, with the number you actually need for each ingredient.

## Why 1 Cup of Flour Is Not 8 Ounces

Flour has roughly a third of the density of water because the space between the particles is air. Scoop flour straight from the bag with the measuring cup and you compress it, pushing that number from 120 g up to 150 g per cup — a 25% error. That is the difference between a tender cake and a dense brick.

Two habits fix it:

- **Spoon and level.** Spoon flour into the cup, then sweep the top flat with a knife. Never tap or pack.
- **Weigh when the recipe gives grams.** If a recipe lists 120 g of flour per cup and you scoop, you are effectively using 1.25 cups. Weights remove the ambiguity entirely, which is why professional formulas almost always use them.

## Fluid Ounces: US, UK, and Metric Are All Different

This is where most cup-to-ounce confusion lives. A "cup" is not an international unit.

| Cup standard | Volume | US fl oz | UK fl oz |
|--------------|--------|----------|----------|
| US customary cup | 236.6 mL | 8.0 | 8.3 |
| US legal cup (nutrition labels) | 240 mL | 8.1 | 8.4 |
| Metric cup (Australia, Canada, NZ) | 250 mL | 8.5 | 8.8 |
| UK imperial cup (legacy) | 284.1 mL | 9.6 | 10.0 |

A UK fluid ounce is 28.41 mL; a US fluid ounce is 29.57 mL. They are close enough that most recipes work, but they are not interchangeable, and a UK cookbook using "1 cup" predating metrication is really asking for about 284 mL — a 20% larger pour than a US cup.

Practical rule for modern recipes: **assume US cup (236.6 mL) unless the recipe or site is Australian, in which case assume 250 mL.** If precision matters, use the [Conversion Chart](/conversion-chart/) and work in grams.

## How to Tell Which Ounce a Recipe Means

Use these signals before you commit:

- **"fl oz," "fluid oz," or a liquid ingredient** → volume. Milk, stock, juice, oil, vanilla.
- **"oz" next to a solid, or a package weight** → weight. "8 oz cream cheese" is one block, not one cup. "8 oz chocolate chips" is about 1⅓ cups.
- **Butter** → weight. A stick is 4 oz, so "8 oz butter" is 2 sticks or 1 cup.
- **Anything with "drained weight"** → weight of the solids only, never a volume.
- **Nutrition labels in the US** → the US legal cup of 240 mL, which is what the serving size is based on.

When in doubt on a liquid, 8 fl oz of water-like liquid is close enough to 8 oz of weight that the recipe will not notice. When in doubt on a powdery solid, do not guess — convert with grams and skip the argument.

## Scaling Cups and Ounces Up and Down

Doubling a recipe is easy in cups (2 cups stay 2 cups) but annoying in ounces (4.2 oz becomes 8.4 oz, which no kitchen scale labels helpfully). Two shortcuts:

- **Work in cups, convert once.** Scale the cup amounts first, then convert the final total. Fewer rounding errors.
- **Switch to grams for anything above 4 cups.** 960 g of flour is unambiguous; "8 cups" depends on who scooped it.
