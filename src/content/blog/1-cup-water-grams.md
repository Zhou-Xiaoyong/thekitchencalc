## 1 Cup of Water in Grams: The Short Answer

**One US cup of water weighs 236 grams** (236.6 g at room temperature, 236.1 g at 20 °C). One metric cup of water weighs **250 grams**. One US legal cup — the one nutrition labels use — holds **240 grams**.

Water is the easiest ingredient in the kitchen to convert, because the conversion is not approximate. It is a definition.

| Cup standard | Volume | Water weight |
|--------------|--------|--------------|
| US customary cup | 236.6 mL | 236 g |
| US legal cup (nutrition labels) | 240 mL | 240 g |
| Metric cup (AU / CA / NZ) | 250 mL | 250 g |
| UK imperial cup (legacy) | 284.1 mL | 284 g |
| ½ US cup | 118.3 mL | 118 g |
| ⅓ US cup | 78.9 mL | 79 g |
| ¼ US cup | 59.1 mL | 59 g |
| 1 US tablespoon | 14.79 mL | 14.8 g |
| 1 US teaspoon | 4.93 mL | 4.9 g |

## Why 1 mL of Water Is (Almost Exactly) 1 Gram

The metric system was built on water. The original definition of the gram was "the mass of one cubic centimeter of pure water at 4 °C," and it is still good to within about 0.002% today. That means for water, **milliliters and grams are the same number** — no density lookup required.

This is why water is the one ingredient where a liquid measuring cup doubles as a scale. Set the cup down, fill to the 236 mL line, and you have 236 g.

The tiny caveat is temperature. Water's density peaks at 4 °C (39 °F) and falls off slowly as it warms:

| Water temperature | Density (g/mL) | 1 US cup (236.6 mL) weighs |
|-------------------|----------------|----------------------------|
| 4 °C (39 °F) | 1.0000 | 236.6 g |
| 20 °C (68 °F) | 0.9982 | 236.2 g |
| 40 °C (104 °F) | 0.9922 | 234.8 g |
| 60 °C (140 °F) | 0.9832 | 232.6 g |
| 100 °C (212 °F, boiling) | 0.9584 | 226.8 g |

A cup of boiling water weighs about 10 g less than a cup of cold water. For recipe purposes, ignore it — a 4% difference in water is far below the noise level of most baking. Where it does matter is when you are measuring hot water by weight and then comparing it against a volume target, such as calibrating a coffee brewing ratio at 200 °F.

## Water in Recipes: Where the Gram Number Actually Gets Used

Water shows up less as "add 1 cup of water" and more as a hidden ratio, and those are the cases where you want the gram figure.

**Bread and pizza dough.** Hydration is expressed as a percentage of flour weight, not a cup count. A 70% hydration dough with 500 g of flour needs 350 g of water — which is **1.48 cups**, not a round 1½ cups. Estimating "about 1½ cups" gives you 355 g, a 1.4% hydration error, which is genuinely fine. Estimating "about 1 cup" gives you 236 g and a 47% hydration dough that will not come together. Run the numbers with the [Dough Hydration Calculator](/dough-hydration-calculator/) rather than guessing.

**Brines and salt solutions.** A 5% brine is 5 g of salt per 95 g of water — by weight, because salt's volume varies wildly between kosher, table, and flake. 1 cup of water (236 g) plus 12.4 g of salt gives you roughly a 5% solution. Weigh the salt, measure the water, and use the [Brine Calculator](/brine-calculator/) to scale to the size of your bird or pork loin.

**Simple syrup and sugar solutions.** A 1:1 simple syrup by volume is 1 cup of water to 1 cup of sugar. By weight it is 236 g of water to 200 g of sugar — not 1:1 at all. Syrup made by the volume method comes out slightly richer, and cocktail recipes written by weight will not match. Use the [Sugar & Salt Calculator](/sugar-salt-calculator/) if you want the two methods to line up.

**Rice and grains.** Most rice cooks at roughly 2:1 water to rice by volume, which is about 236 g of water per 185 g of dry rice. Doubling a batch means doubling both, and the pot size becomes the limiting factor long before the ratio does.

**Gelatin and starches.** Blooming gelatin calls for cold water in a specific multiple of the gelatin's weight — commonly 5 parts water to 1 part gelatin. 10 g of gelatin needs 50 g of water, which is **3.4 tablespoons**, not a quarter cup (59 g, an 18% overshoot).

## Measuring 236 Grams of Water Without a Scale

Since the density is 1, the conversion is trivial:

- **1 US cup = 236 g.** Fill a liquid measuring cup to the 1-cup / 8 fl oz line.
- **½ US cup = 118 g.** The 4 fl oz line.
- **250 g (metric) = 250 mL.** The 1-cup line on an Australian or Canadian jug.
- **240 g (US legal cup) = 240 mL.** What a US nutrition label means by "1 cup."
- **1 fl oz = 29.6 g.** Useful for small amounts.
- **1 tbsp = 14.8 g; 1 tsp = 4.9 g.** For any quantity under a quarter cup.

Read liquid measurements at eye level, with the cup on a flat surface, and check the bottom of the meniscus rather than the top of the curve — that curve is worth about 2–3 mL, or roughly 2 g.

For a full recipe, be consistent: measure the water in the same cup every time. Mixing a dry cup for flour and a liquid cup for water is fine, but mixing a US cup for one ingredient and a metric cup for another is not.

## Scaling Water Quantities in a Recipe

Water scales linearly — no tricks, no leavening interactions — but three things around it do not:

- **Evaporation.** Doubling a simmer time does not double the evaporation; it roughly quadruples the surface area you expose over that time only if you also widen the pot. A doubled stew in the same narrow pot often finishes with more liquid than you expect.
- **Pan surface area.** If you scale a recipe up, check the pan with the [Baking Pan Converter](/baking-pan-converter/) before you commit. A water bath recipe scaled to a larger pan needs its own volume checked too — a 9×13 pan holds about 3 quarts, and the water bath needs to come halfway up the sides.
- **Dissolved solids.** If the water carries salt or sugar, the *solution's* weight is higher than the water alone. 1 cup of 5% brine weighs about 248 g, not 236 g. When you scale a brine, scale the salt and the water separately rather than the finished solution.
