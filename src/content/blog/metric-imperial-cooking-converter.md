## Metric to Imperial: The Four Conversions That Matter

"Converting metric to imperial" is not one operation — it is four, and they use different maths. Get them separated in your head and the whole page becomes simple:

- **Weight:** 1 oz = 28.35 g, 1 lb = 453.6 g
- **Volume:** 1 US cup = 236.6 mL, 1 US fl oz = 29.57 mL
- **Temperature:** °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32
- **Length (pan sizes):** 1 inch = 2.54 cm

Only the first two involve ingredient-dependent judgement. The last two are pure arithmetic, and the temperature one is the only conversion in the kitchen where an error is a food-safety problem rather than a texture problem.

## The Multiplier Shortcuts

For everyday cooking, three multipliers cover almost everything:

- **Ounces to grams:** × 28 (or × 30 when you want a fast estimate)
- **Grams to ounces:** ÷ 28
- **Cups to millilitres:** × 240 (the US cup rounded up from 236.6)
- **Millilitres to cups:** ÷ 240
- **°C to °F:** × 2, then add 30 — accurate to within about 10°F in the 150–220°C baking range

That last shortcut is the one people get wrong most often. 180°C × 2 = 360, + 30 = 390°F. The real answer is 356°F, and 350°F is what most recipes call for. Real-world ovens swing more than 30°F anyway, so the shortcut is fine for setting the dial — but if you are checking a probe thermometer against a recipe's target internal temperature, use the exact formula. ## Why One Multiplier Never Works for Both Flour and Water

This is the part that trips up metric-to-imperial conversion more than anything else: **a cup is a volume, a gram is a weight, and the bridge between them depends entirely on the ingredient.**

| Ingredient | 1 US cup | 1 metric cup (250 mL) |
|------------|----------|----------------------|
| Water | 237 g | 250 g |
| All-purpose flour | 120 g | 127 g |
| Granulated sugar | 200 g | 211 g |
| Brown sugar, packed | 213 g | 225 g |
| Butter, solid | 227 g | 239 g |
| Rolled oats | 90 g | 95 g |
| Honey | 340 g | 358 g |

So when a European recipe says "250 g flour" and you reach for an imperial cup measure, you are not looking for one cup — you are looking for about 2 cups, because flour is light. And a US recipe's "1 cup of honey" is not 250 g in any system; it is 340 g. The lesson is the same in both directions: **convert weight to weight and volume to volume, and only cross the boundary with an ingredient-specific density.**

## Converting a Whole Recipe: Do It Once, Not Line by Line

The most common mistake with metric-to-imperial cooking is converting measurements mid-cook. You read "180 g of butter," convert to 6.35 oz, then convert the ounces to cups, and by the third ingredient you have introduced three rounding errors and lost track of which lines are already converted.

A better workflow:

1. **Pick one system and commit.** If your scale reads grams, convert the whole recipe to grams before you start. If you cook by cups, convert the whole recipe to cups.
2. **Convert weights with the scale, volumes with the chart.** Never convert a weight into a volume unless the recipe is a simple liquid.
3. **Write it down.** Rewrite the ingredient list on a notepad or a card in the target units. Future you will thank present you.
4. **Convert temperatures last.** They are independent of the ingredients, so they can be done at the oven.
5. **Check the pan.** Pan dimensions convert separately, and a 20 cm tin is not the same as an 8-inch tin — it is 20.32 cm, but the two are treated as equivalent because that is how the industries standardised.

## The Traps Worth Knowing About

**The US fluid ounce versus the imperial fluid ounce.** A US fl oz is 29.57 mL; a UK imperial fl oz is 28.41 mL. They differ by 4%, and old British cookbooks use the imperial one. A pint is worse: 473 mL in the US, 568 mL in the UK. If a recipe from a 1970s British book calls for a pint of stock, use 568 mL.

**Cups are not 250 mL everywhere.** US and Japanese cups are ~237 mL (the Japanese one is exactly 200 mL for rice). Australian, Canadian, and New Zealand cups are 250 mL. A recipe using 4 cups is therefore anywhere between 800 mL and 1 L.

**Ounce confusion is twofold — weight or volume.** "8 oz of cheese" is a weight (227 g); "8 fl oz of milk" is a volume (237 mL). Recipes that skip the "fl" are usually talking about weight, but not always, and that is exactly why the gram column matters.

**Spoons differ too.** A US tablespoon is 14.79 mL, a British metric tablespoon is 15 mL, and an Australian tablespoon is 20 mL. In a small batch of vinaigrette, that is a measurable difference in acidity.
